# Changelog archive

The long-form record of every Canari release. [`CHANGELOG.md`](../CHANGELOG.md) at the root carries
the condensed entry for each change; this file carries the full account behind it - what a user saw,
what caused it, and what was decided. Nothing here summarises something written elsewhere: for every
release up to and including v0.13.1, this is the only copy.

Entries are ordered newest release first, and within a release by the Keep a Changelog groups.

## How v0.11.8 to v0.13.1 were reconstructed

Every change released between 2026-07-31 and 2026-08-07 had been appended to a single
`[Unreleased]` section and never split, so four tags shipped with no changelog section naming them.
Each entry was attributed by matching its subject against the commits in its own tag range
(`git log v0.13.0..v0.13.1` and its three neighbours), **not** by its position in the file: a commit
authored before a tag can still be tagged after it, and one entry here - the Android app claiming the
`canari-emse.fr` URL the browser was waiting for, authored 2026-08-05 - sits on the later side of
exactly that boundary and belongs to v0.13.1 rather than v0.13.0. Where an entry matched no commit
subject it took the version of the entries appended beside it, which holds because a block was
appended by one session at one time.

## v0.11.3 has a tag and no section

This gap is older than the reconstruction above and was not filled, because filling it would mean
writing changelog prose that never existed rather than moving prose that does. `v0.11.3`
(`cd1ac978`, 2026-07-28) shipped seven commits - the external-commit joiner registered as a group
member and no longer replayed pre-join messages, the MLS persistence and sync logs put back in
English, and the repo `oxfmt` profile restored. `git log v0.11.2..v0.11.3` is the record of it.

## [0.14.0] - 2026-08-17

### Added
- **You can now scroll back past what your device kept.** A browser keeps the last ninety days of a conversation and a phone or desktop app keeps five years - so on the web, scrolling far enough up used to reach the bottom of what the browser had and simply stop, with nothing to say whether that was the beginning of the conversation or the end of what this device happened to keep. Reaching that point now asks another of the conversation's devices for the page before it, automatically, and tells you what is happening: a loading row while the request is out, and a plain message when nobody was online to answer - which is a different thing from "there is nothing older", and used to look identical. Each request brings back one page and scrolling further asks again, so a conversation of any size is walked the same way; nothing is ever asked for below the point the conversation itself is allowed to forget
- **A device that asks for more history than the one answering it has kept now goes and asks somebody else.** A browser keeps the last ninety days of a conversation and a phone keeps five years, deliberately - the browser should not pay the phone's storage cost. The consequence had never been handled: whenever a phone asked a browser to catch it up, the answer came back covering ninety days and stopping there, every single time, by design and not by fault. The phone could not tell that apart from "this conversation has no more past", because both look like nothing arriving - so it either concluded it was complete, and quietly did without years of messages, or asked again, which is worse: the same device gets picked, gives the same answer, and only a timer could ever end it. The answering device now says where its own memory begins, and says it only when that is later than what was asked for, so two devices of the same kind still exchange a single message between them. The asking device then asks another member of the conversation, skipping the ones that have already answered, and stops when the server reports there is nobody left it has not already heard from - which is a fact, delivered by the one party that knows it, rather than a delay waited out. Each round rules out exactly one device, so the search visits each at most once and ends. It also covers the case nothing else would have caught: two devices can hold exactly the same messages over the range being compared and both be missing the years below - they agree perfectly, and agreeing is not the same as being complete
- **Deleting your account now deletes the photos and files you uploaded, which it never did.** The account row went, the messages went, the posts went - the media stayed on disk for ever, because nothing on the server knew they were yours: it stores your files as encrypted blocks it cannot read, and the one piece of identity it had, the token proving you were allowed to upload, was checked and then thrown away. It is kept now, and account deletion asks the media server to remove everything uploaded under it. Two limits are deliberate. Files uploaded **before this change** carry no owner and cannot be matched to anyone, so they are still only reachable by the 30-day expiry - there is no way to work out after the fact who sent what. And association logos are left alone, since they belong to the association and outlive whoever happened to upload one. Deleting a single **message** still does not delete its file, on purpose: forwarding a photo copies the reference to it, so the same file can be shown in conversations the person deleting cannot see, and an end-to-end encrypted server cannot count who is still pointing at what - deleting it would silently break someone else's conversation. Those files stop being opened and the 30-day expiry takes them.
- **The text of a PDF opened in the app can now be selected and copied.** Reported from use: "on ne peut pas selectionner le texte, tout a ete transforme en image". That was exactly true, and the first half of it has to stay - the reader draws each page as an image because that is the only renderer Android's WebView has, and an embedded PDF is simply blank there. But turning a page into an image only costs you its text if nothing puts the text back, and that is a gap rather than a price: the real characters are now laid over the image, invisible, each one positioned where its glyphs are, which is how a PDF reader in a browser has always done it. Selecting, copying and the browser's own "find in page" work over a document the way they work over any other text. Two things make it stay aligned while you zoom, which redraws the image underneath it: every position is recorded as a fraction of the page rather than in pixels, so one extraction serves every zoom level, every window width and every screen density with nothing recomputed; and each run of text is stretched onto the width the document says it occupies, because the browser draws it in a substituted font and the selection highlight follows the text rather than the image - without that correction the highlight drifts further from what you can see with every word. Text rotated on the page (a watermark down a margin, typically) is placed correctly rather than collapsing to nothing, and a run whose position cannot be worked out is left out entirely rather than dumped in the top-left corner, where it would be selectable text belonging nowhere. Searching ACROSS a document, jumping between matches, is a separate feature and is not included; the extraction this adds is what it will be built on
- **A PDF attached to a post or a message can now be read in the app - tap it anywhere, on any platform.** Until now the only thing you could do with a document was download it, and the preview under a post was decoration: clicking the page did nothing, and clicking the file name did nothing either. The whole card is now the target - the header row and the preview alike, which is how anyone reads it - and it opens a reader that renders the document page by page. It renders rather than embeds because that is the only portable option: chat and post media are encrypted with a per-file key before upload, so the server holds an opaque blob and can neither thumbnail it nor serve a viewer for it, and an `<iframe>` over the decrypted file is simply blank on Android, whose WebView ships no PDF renderer at all. One rendering path therefore serves web, Android, iOS and desktop identically - the same reasoning that already shaped the first-page thumbnails, now shared with them through `utils/pdfDocument.ts`. Pages rasterise as they scroll into view, so opening a two-hundred-page document costs one page, and zooming re-renders at the new size rather than magnifying a bitmap, which is the difference between readable and not on a phone. Two subtleties are worth recording because they are invisible until they bite: the effects that own the page bitmaps must depend on the document and the zoom **only** - reading the page list inside them makes each arriving page revoke the images already on screen - and a page that is already in view produces no new intersection event, so after a zoom the visible pages have to be asked for explicitly or the viewer stays blank
- **A link in a message or a post now warns you before opening it if Google Safe Browsing has flagged it as malware, phishing or unwanted software.** Until now `AppLink` opened whatever it was given with zero safety check - a dangerous link pasted into a conversation read identically to a legitimate one. The check is server-side, since a Safe Browsing API key is a secret that can never ship client-side: a new `GET /api/mls/link-safety` endpoint in `chat-delivery-service` looks the URL up and answers `{ unsafe: boolean }`, cached in its own in-memory `TtlCache` (the same pattern already used for link previews, and deliberately its own cache and its own endpoint rather than a field bolted onto the existing preview response - a page with a broken `<title>` already makes the preview fetch throw, and folding the safety verdict into that response would have taken it down with it). The warning shows only at the point of an actual click on a link that came back flagged - a confirm dialog you can still choose to dismiss - never a badge decorating every rendered link, which would be alert fatigue for a check that is almost always going to say "fine". Fails open at every layer: no API key configured, a network error, a timeout, or Google itself being unreachable all mean the link opens exactly as before, since a safety check that is down must never become an outage for every link in the app - but a failure is never remembered, only a real verdict is, because caching "could not ask" would turn one dropped request into "this link is never checked again" for as long as the page lives, which on mobile is days. Verified against the real API from the deployed server (whose outbound IP the key is restricted to) with Google's own test malware URL, confirming a flagged response carries the exact shape the code expects
- **A message or a post naming `canari-emse.fr`, `gallery.mitv.fr`, or anything under `emse.fr` now becomes a real link even without typing `https://` in front of it.** Every other bare word stays plain text - chat only ever linkified a URL that already carried its scheme, and a post's markdown renderer autolinks a `www.`-prefixed host or an email address but nothing else, so the school's own domains typed the way people actually type them (no scheme) were dead text on both surfaces. A general "known extension" allowlist was tried first and worked, but was set aside for something narrower: French inclusive writing and elided forms make TLD-shape alone an unreliable signal ("cher.es" reads exactly like a Spanish domain, "auteur.rice" and "cher.e.s" are two-letter-TLD-shaped by accident), so rather than trying to out-narrow that ambiguity, an exact whitelist of real hosts sidesteps it entirely. Chat and posts share one regex, so neither surface can drift from the other, and it runs only over text a `https://` URL has not already claimed, so it can never re-linkify part of a link someone already typed in full
- **Settings now shows how much of your own device Canari is using, and lets you clear the reclaimable part.** Nothing on the client measured this before: the media, avatar and association-logo caches, the local message database and - on the app, not the browser - the encrypted key state (`mls.bin`) all accumulate quietly over months of use with no way to see or act on any of it. A new panel breaks the total into what it actually is (media cache, messages and local data, and the encryption state where that can be measured separately) and offers a single "clear media cache" action, scoped to exactly the caches that are safe to lose because everything in them is re-fetched from the server on demand. That action has no path to messages, `mls.bin` or any device key - it never lists the app's data directory at all, it only deletes three named caches by name. On the app, the local database and key-state sizes come from a small Rust command that reads real file sizes on disk; in a browser, where no API reports that breakdown, the total comes from the origin's own storage estimate with the measured cache subtracted out.
- **iOS gets its own dedicated login browser session, closing the gap WP-OIDC-TAB-1 left open on that platform.** That fix gave Android a Chrome Custom Tab that the OS closes automatically once the OIDC deep link brings the app back; iOS kept the plain system-browser launch, since its own equivalent - `ASWebAuthenticationSession` - was a separate native surface not built at the time. `tauri-plugin-customtabs` now has an `ios/` implementation (mirroring `patches/tauri-plugin-keystore`'s Swift Package Manager layout) using exactly that. The one part that isn't a straight mirror of Android: `ASWebAuthenticationSession` intercepts its own callback instead of handing it to the app's normal URL-opening path, so the plugin re-opens that callback via `UIApplication.open(_:)` to route it back through the same shared deep-link pipeline (`hooks.client.ts`, `/auth/callback`) Android already uses - standard behavior for an app re-invoking its own URL scheme, but unverified here specifically, since this repo has never run an iOS build on a device or simulator. That single mechanism is the first thing to check once one exists
- **The admin panel now has a Storage page reporting what the SERVER itself is using: disk, database, media objects and cache.** No such measurement previously ran anywhere - every number ever quoted about server storage came from one-off SSH commands during a research session. A new endpoint measures the four independently (the whole Postgres database, the MinIO bucket's total size and object count via a server-to-server call to media-service, Redis memory, and the container's own disk) and reports whichever succeed even when one fails, rather than letting one unreachable dependency blank the other three. The admin navigation was also reworked while adding it: nine flat tabs in a horizontally-scrolling bar become three dropdown groups (Moderation, Community, Platform) plus two direct links (Cercle, Storage), each group rendering only the sub-pages the current admin can actually open.
- **If the app ever stops receiving messages entirely, it now says so instead of going quiet.** Incoming messages are processed one at a time, and the app will not start a second pass while one is running - which is correct, and which means that if any single step of that pass never finishes, every message that arrives afterwards is put in a queue that nobody will ever empty, for as long as the app stays open. That has happened twice for two unrelated reasons, and both times the log said nothing at all: there was no line distinguishing "still working" from "stuck for ever", so the only symptom was a conversation that had simply stopped. Each was fixed where it was found; the shape was left open, and nothing prevented the next one. There is now a single way to wait inside that pass, and it reports itself after a minute and every minute after that, naming which of the four steps is stuck, which conversation and which message - the elapsed time being the actual diagnosis, since one slow minute and twenty are different problems. It reports rather than abandoning the step, and that is deliberate: giving up on the last step would let a second pass start on top of the first while it is still writing what it decrypted, which loses messages outright. A freeze loses nothing permanently. The older watchdog covering just one of the four steps is removed, since it would now report the same thing twice while still ignoring the other three
- **The server now says, every hour, how much undelivered mail it is holding and which device is holding the most of it.** It deletes nothing - it only looks. The reason it exists is that on 10 August one device quietly accumulated 28 124 undelivered messages in five hours, thirty times the rest of the platform put together, and nothing anywhere said so; it was found by hand a day later, by which point the table had grown to 70 MB. Every mechanism involved had behaved exactly as designed - the messages were within their retention window, and the device still looked perfectly valid - so nothing was broken enough to complain. What was missing was somebody looking. The report names the five deepest queues and, for any device past a threshold set from what production actually measures (roughly twenty-four times the largest healthy backlog, and a tenth of what that incident produced in one hour), warns with the date that device last connected alongside it - because the depth alone cannot distinguish a live device that is failing to keep up, which is a bug to chase, from one that will never come back, which is debris awaiting collection, and those call for opposite responses. Deliberately not a cap: refusing to hold more mail for a device would trade a disk problem for silent message loss, which is the one thing this service exists to prevent
- **The photos and files stored on the server are now backed up in a way that does not multiply them.** Every night the server made a fresh full copy of every media file and kept fourteen of them, which means each file stored costs fifteen times its size on disk - and because these files are encrypted before they leave your device, they cannot be compressed and never change once written. There is now a second, deduplicating backup running alongside the existing one: it stores each file once and adds only what is genuinely new. Measured on the server the night it was installed, a second run on an unchanged set of files added 24 KB rather than another 45 MB. It was also verified the only way a backup can honestly be verified - by restoring it and comparing every restored file against the original: 172 media files, byte for byte identical. The existing backup is untouched and remains the reference until the change is reviewed.
- **First step of the Stripe -> Lydia payment migration (WP-LYDIA-1): a provider abstraction, with Stripe behind it unchanged.** `core-service`'s payment module talked to the Stripe SDK directly from `PaymentService`, so swapping payment providers would have meant editing the one class every checkout, onboarding and saved-card code path called into. A `PaymentProvider` interface now sits between them; `StripePaymentProvider` is a pure extraction of what was already there - same Stripe calls, same behavior, verified by the existing test suite passing unchanged. A `LydiaPaymentProvider` implements the two flows that map cleanly onto that interface today (one-off checkout via `request/do`, session lookup via `request/state`). Which provider is live is an admin-editable setting (default stays Stripe, so nothing changes in production yet). Everything else - association onboarding, live balance/status, saved payment methods - throws a clear, documented error instead of faking a result: Lydia has no live status-poll endpoint (only a one-shot `BUSINESS_VALIDATED` webhook), and saved payment methods are being retired outright rather than reimplemented (every purchase becomes its own interactive request). Full mapping and open questions in the WP-LYDIA-1 plan
- **Association onboarding now supports Lydia's `business/create`, which needs the club's legal profile upfront.** Stripe's hosted onboarding link let the club fill in its own name, address and contact details on Stripe's page; Lydia has no such flow, so Canari collects it itself. The association edit page now asks `GET /api/payments/provider` which processor is active and renders either the existing Stripe button (unchanged) or a new form (name, address, city, country, contact email/phone) that calls `business/create` directly. The Business's own private token is deliberately never stored - provider-signed calls use Canari's own provider token, confirmed by the `business/addcashier` signature spec
- **The Stripe/Lydia switch lives in the platform admin settings, not an environment variable.** Flipping it in `/admin/platform` takes effect immediately, no deploy or restart - `PlatformService` already reads its config straight from Postgres on every call (same as `maintenanceEnabled`/`minClientVersion`), so `PaymentService` now asks it, per call, which provider is live instead of fixing one at startup from an env var. Both providers are still built once from their env-held secrets (`STRIPE_SECRET_KEY`, `LYDIA_PROVIDER_TOKEN`/`_PRIVATE_TOKEN`) - only the choice of which one is active moved to the database
- **Lydia's credentials are wired through the same infra as Stripe's, but core-service only.** `LYDIA_ENV`/`LYDIA_PROVIDER_TOKEN`/`LYDIA_PROVIDER_PRIVATE_TOKEN` are in `infrastructure/.env.example`, all three docker-compose files (core-service's environment block only - unlike Stripe's, which also reaches social-service unused), both CD workflows (secret sync + upsert into the server's `.env`, and the prod post-deploy drift check that fingerprints the value actually inside the running container). Actual token values still need to come from Lydia (Livrable A, WP-LYDIA-1 plan) and be set as GitHub secrets - this only makes the pipeline ready to carry them

### Changed
- **Canari is on the Play Store, so it stops asking people to update - and the one prompt that remains now leads somewhere that works.** A non-dismissible modal opened on every launch as soon as the client was a single patch behind the deployed server, with "Plus tard" as its only exit. On a store-distributed app that is pure friction: the store updates people by itself, and interrupting them to say so is a worse experience than saying nothing. It is deleted outright, and the installed version moves to a discreet "A propos" block in `/settings` - which also names a newer version when there is one, without blocking anything. `minClientVersion` from `/admin/platform` becomes the **only** thing that can interrupt a user, which makes the field the whole escalation ladder in one step; the admin wiki and the French user guide now both carry the timing trap that comes with it, since the gate applies the instant it is saved while a Play review plus rollout takes days - raise it before the new build is actually installable and every mobile user is locked out behind a button leading to a store still serving the old version. The blocking gate itself was also lying: `PlatformGateOverlay` hardcoded "le telechargement de l'APK s'ouvrira dans votre navigateur" for every Android user, and the update destination was a build-time env var (`VITE_ANDROID_PLAY_STORE_URL`, `VITE_IOS_APP_STORE_URL`) fed by GitHub repo variables **that were never set** - so Android pointed at the GitHub APK regardless and the iOS prompt was hidden entirely. The URLs are now constants (the App Store one geo-neutral, so Apple redirects to the reader's own storefront), the env plumbing is deleted from both release workflows, and an unset variable can no longer mean "update path silently dead". But the destination could not simply become a constant either, because of the trap that shaped the whole change: the workflow uploads an `.aab`, so the Play binary is re-signed by **Google Play App Signing** while the APK on the GitHub release keeps our upload key - **neither can install over the other**, and switching sides requires an uninstall, which wipes `mls.bin`, the keystore entry and the entire local history. Sending a sideloader to Play is therefore not a slightly wrong link but a dead end ending in data loss. So the target is resolved at runtime from how this install actually arrived: `CanariApplication` records `packageManager.getInstallSourceInfo(...).installingPackageName` at startup into `installer_package.txt` (with the `getInstallerPackageName` fallback below API 30, `minSdk` being under it), a new `get_installer_package` Tauri command reads it back, and `com.android.vending` means Play while anything else means the APK. The file hop follows the existing `push_context.json` pattern rather than introducing a plugin, which would force `gen/android/tauri.settings.gradle` to be regenerated and risk the hand-maintained `AndroidManifest.xml`; nothing type-checks a cross-process path, so `installerPackageContract.test.ts` pins the filename, the directory helper, the `generate_handler!` registration and the installer package literal, exactly as `fcmCacheFields.test.ts` does for the FCM cache. The decision itself (`buildUpdateTarget`) is a pure function of platform plus install source, unit-tested across the five cases; the probe is the thin async wrapper around it, memoised, and a failed probe assumes Play **and logs a warning** rather than swallowing it - every build shipping this code writes the file, so a miss is a fault, not a state. iOS is prepared ahead of its release and always goes to the App Store, having no sideloadable binary to disagree about
- **The status banners no longer say the same thing twice, no longer shift the page under your finger, and are now readable.** Six of them had been written independently and agreed on nothing. Two pairs were saying one thing twice: going offline raised "Hors ligne" straight away and "En attente de connexion" three seconds later, and a synchronisation raised one banner above the whole app and a second one over the conversation, both from the same flag - so they never appeared apart. Four of the six were translucent, which put the text underneath behind the words meant to be read. And the two that sat above the application were in the layout flow, so each appearance pushed everything down and each disappearance snapped it back - 29 px, measured, arriving 480 ms into a page load and leaving at 2 286 ms, which is exactly when someone is reaching for the sidebar: a click aimed at a channel row was recorded landing on the "Ajouter un canal" button below it. The duplicates are gone (connectivity is announced once, at the window; a synchronisation once, where the messages actually arrive), every banner now draws on an opaque surface from one token, and the two window-scale banners share a single column so a maintenance notice can no longer paint over a fatal encryption error. The synchronisation banner is also bound to real messages arriving rather than to startup, where it had been announcing a synchronisation of nothing at all
- **Deleting a device now takes effect on that device immediately, and leaves nothing of it behind.** Removing a device from the security page purged everything the server held for it, and the device itself noticed none of that: its session stayed open and everything it held locally stayed on it, until whenever it next signed in. For a phone its owner has just declared lost, "it will find out eventually" is the wrong answer to what they asked. The server now tells the device the moment it is deleted, and the device signs out and returns itself to the state of a brand-new install - not only the encryption keys, but the conversation list, the cached photos, the drafts, the preferences, the fingerprint unlock, all of it. Two deliberate details: the device checks with the server before erasing anything, because a message arriving over the network is not on its own a reason to destroy a phone's contents - and if that check cannot reach the server, the answer is "not revoked", so a connection problem can never wipe anybody. The record kept by the server remains the reliable half; this only makes it immediate
- **Android notifications now speak the language you chose in Canari, everywhere.** Canari's settings have a Français/English switch, and the phone has its own - two different settings, and only one of them is you telling this app what to speak. On an English phone, most of what Android showed was English already, from a translation table the app has always had. But nineteen sentences had never been put in that table and were written into the program in French: the notification that a synchronisation is waiting, the one saying messages are still queued, the generic "you received an encrypted message", the "new message in #salon", the word naming your own messages in a conversation - and, more visibly than any of them, the names and descriptions of the five notification categories, which are what the Android settings screen lists when you go to turn one of them off. All nineteen are now in the table, in both languages. The iPhone side had been done in August and, together with it, this closes the whole client half of the problem - what is left belongs to the server, which does not know anyone's language and should not be writing these sentences at all. Two details worth knowing: a notification category keeps the wording it was created with, so switching language does not rename the categories you already have - re-creating them would throw away the sound and importance you picked, which is the worse trade. And a new automatic check now reads the program's own source and refuses a sentence that was added in one language and forgotten in the other, in either platform - the failure that has no other symptom, since the app simply falls back to French and the build stays green
- **On iPhone, six things were still written in French whatever language you chose, and the notification buttons now follow that choice.** The August work put the notification SENTENCES in a table in both languages and left every LABEL behind: the three buttons on a message notification (Répondre, Envoyer, Marquer comme lu), the word standing in for a salon whose name did not reach the phone, and the two lines announcing an incoming call. All six are now in the table, on both halves of the app - the app itself and the small helper that runs when it is fully closed, which is a separate program with its own copy. One of them behaves better than its Android counterpart, and it is worth knowing why: an Android notification category keeps the wording it was created with for ever, so switching language does not rename the ones you already have, whereas iPhone replaces the whole set of buttons each time it is told to - so Canari re-registers them whenever it notices your language has changed. It does that as the app leaves the foreground, which is not a delay or a periodic check but the last moment before a notification can appear at all: you cannot see one while you are looking at the app, so the buttons cannot be shown in the old language after the change. What is still true, and said plainly: none of this has run on a real iPhone, only compiled - the machine this was written on is not a Mac
- **A salon notification now says which community it comes from.** It used to read `#general` and nothing else, which tells you almost nothing when two communities you belong to both have a `#general` - and they do. The title is now the community, then the salon: `Campagne de test - #general`. Worth a word on why this was not simply a display change: the community's name was not on the phone to be shown. The notification carried the community's internal identifier, which is a number, and no part of a phone can turn one into a name - there is nothing on the device that lists them. So the name itself now travels with the notification, and the four separate programs that can put one of these on a screen - the server, Android, and the two halves of the iPhone app - each spell the title out for themselves, with an automatic check holding the four to the same wording. If the community somehow cannot be named, the notification still arrives, reading `#general` exactly as before, and the server records that loudly rather than letting the name quietly stop appearing
- **A photo, video or document is now kept for thirty days after somebody last opened it - which is what the rule always claimed, and was not.** Files are deleted from the server thirty days after their last use, and the server measured that by the last time it was asked for the file. The app, however, keeps a copy of every file it has already downloaded, so once your phone has an image it never asks again: a photo everybody in a conversation opens daily left the server exactly the same trace as one nobody ever opened twice - the first download - and both were deleted on the same day. The consequence was invisible and only hit people who had done nothing wrong: someone joining on a new phone, or reinstalling, would find nothing older than a month, including files their friends were still looking at every day. The app now tells the server when it uses a file it already had, so the clock follows real use. It stays cheap by carrying only what is actually new: a file is reported at most once a day, since the deadline is measured in months and a second report the same day says nothing, and all the reports from one moment travel together in a single message. If that message fails to arrive, nothing is remembered as sent, so the next time you open the file it is reported again - the one thing this must never do is shorten a deadline it was meant to extend
- **The image viewer and the PDF reader are now one interface, and a PDF can be dragged around with the mouse.** They always did the same job - take over the screen, name what you are looking at, offer to download it, close - and they were two separate pieces of code doing it, which is how they had quietly drifted apart: the two close buttons were not even labelled the same way for a screen reader, one of them along with the "next image" arrow and the image counter having been left untranslated in French only. Everything they share is now written once, so they cannot drift again, and the three untranslated labels are translated. What is deliberately *not* shared is the area the content lives in, because the two are genuinely different: a photo is a single image that must never scroll, so the viewer takes over every touch on it, whereas a PDF is a column of pages you read by scrolling, and taking over the same touches would have made it impossible to read. The zoom arithmetic is shared instead as plain, tested maths - the two viewers need two different models of it, one for scaling a single image about a point and one for a column of pages that are re-drawn sharp at each zoom level, and keeping both in one place with a note on why they are not interchangeable is what stops the wrong one being reached for. The visible gain is that a PDF zoomed past its window can now be dragged with the mouse to move around it, which previously only worked with a finger or by finding the scrollbar; dragging over text still selects the text, as it should
- **The back links across the app all use the same icon now.** "Retour aux associations", "Retour aux listes", "Retour a la page publique" and the two calendar ones were split between a lucide `ArrowLeft` and a literal `←` character typed into the template - and two of them had the arrow baked into the translated string itself, which makes it a piece of layout no translator should be carrying. Every one of them is now the icon, and the strings are text again
- **The chat interface says out loud what it used to only show.** The status banners, the channel rows and the synchronisation notice carried their meaning in colour, position and icons alone, so a screen reader was told none of it: no announcement that the app had gone offline or started synchronising, and a channel row that read as "general 3" with nothing to say the 3 was unread messages, the lock meant private, or which channel was the open one. Every banner is now a live region, an interrupting one only where the message genuinely cannot wait; channel rows carry a full name, their unread count in words and a marker for the current one; decorative icons and badges are hidden from assistive tech instead of being read out as noise
- **Whether your message was sent or read is now said, not just drawn.** The state of your last message was a single tick for "sent" and two ticks plus the readers' avatars for "read" - a difference of one stroke and one colour, and nothing else. Without sight the two were identical, which is to say absent. Both now carry a spoken label, and the decorative half - the avatars, the "+2", the double tick - is hidden from assistive technology so it is not announced as a row of unnamed images ahead of the sentence that says what actually happened
- **"X ecrit..." is now announced by screen readers.** The line telling you the other person is typing was purely visual: it appeared and disappeared with no announcement, so anyone not looking at that corner of the screen never knew. It is now a live region, and the part of it that carries no meaning - the three bouncing dots - is hidden from assistive technology rather than read out
- **The people-search dropdown is now announced properly by screen readers.** It gained the standard roles and states for this kind of field, so assistive technology says when suggestions appear, how many there are, and which one the arrow keys are on. Previously it was a plain list with no announcement at all, and the highlighted entry was conveyed by colour alone
- **The two side panels now have names for screen readers.** The icon rail and the conversation list are both side regions, and neither was named - so the list of landmarks a screen reader offers for jumping around the app contained two entries that read identically and gave no way to tell which was which. The conversation panel's name also follows what it is showing, since the same panel lists either conversations or communities
- **A message could be sent at an out-of-date encryption epoch because an unrelated conversation happened to be loading its history.** Before sending anything queued, a device waits for everything already received to be applied - that wait is the one thing standing between a reconnect and a message encrypted against a state the others have moved past, which nobody can then read. That wait was being ABANDONED whenever any conversation, anywhere in the app, had a history session open: the check asked "is a session open" when the only situation it must refuse is "am I inside the session I would be waiting on". Somebody else's session ends by itself, so waiting simply takes a little longer, which is all the caller ever asked for. Measured on production on 2026-08-16: on one routine reconnect, both the connection's own synchronisation and the pending-message flush were abandoned 98 ms after an unrelated conversation started reading its archive. The check now asks the caller which session it could be inside - a fact each one knows, and now passes as an argument rather than leaving it to be guessed from a label - refuses only that one, and waits out every other. It had been guessing from the label for a day, during which no call site spelled a group id at all, so the deadlock it exists to catch could not have been recognised either
- **A device repairing its history now says how far back it wants, and gets exactly that.** When a device notices it is missing messages it asks the others, which compare their copy against its list and send back the difference - and until now that difference was everything they held, however far back. That works while every device keeps everything, and it is why nothing had ever needed to say otherwise. It stops working the moment they do not: a browser is not the place to accumulate five years of a phone's history, and a device rebuilt from a peer had no way of saying so. Every request now carries the point it wants history from, and the answering device sends nothing older - a browser asks for the last three months, a phone or the desktop app for five years. Three things about it are deliberate. The asker states that point rather than the answerer working it out: it moves forward continuously, so two devices calculating it a second apart would disagree by whatever was sent in between, and one exchange has to use one number. Each side states its own: the same exchange repairs both devices, so a phone answering a browser still asks back for its own five years rather than being capped at the browser's three months. And a copy nobody asked for is not trimmed at all - the history handed to someone being added to a conversation was requested by no one and has no window to honour, and sending too much there costs disk space where sending too little would lose messages that person has no other way to obtain
- **Two devices of the same account now compare what they hold before one sends its history to the other, instead of shipping the whole conversation blind.** A device asking for history used to get everything the answering device had, every time - which is a lot of ciphertext for a phone that was missing three messages, and nothing at all for a phone that was missing one the answerer had already pruned. The exchange is now a difference. The device that ASKS broadcasts a digest of what it holds inside MLS (the server must never see it), the elected answerer computes the gap and replies with exactly the missing messages - and, when the asker turns out to hold messages the answerer lacks, pulls those back in the same exchange. The digest has two shapes so it stays small at any history size: under a thousand messages it is the plain list of ids, above that the messages are divided into slices and each slice carries a count and a truncated hash - a few kilobytes for a conversation of any size. Which slice a message falls into is decided by its **identifier**, never by its date, and that is the whole correctness of it: a date is a value the two devices do not actually agree on (a message can carry the sender's clock on one device and the server's on the other, which puts it either side of midnight), so slicing by date would make two devices disagree about a message near a boundary and re-send its whole period on every exchange, forever, never reaching the "nothing left to exchange" state that ends the repair. An identifier is the same string everywhere by construction. Two further details matter. The ids inside a slice are sorted by codepoint rather than `localeCompare`, since the sort feeds the hash and a locale-dependent comparator would make every slice disagree. And a slice whose hash differs is requested in **both** directions: a fingerprint proves the slice is not identical, never which side is short, so guessing would silently drop messages while over-asking costs only bandwidth (the receiver already dedupes on the message id). How finely the store is divided is chosen from its size and travels with the digest, so the two devices compare the same regions rather than each choosing their own. The two halves of a solicitation travel by different transports - the WebSocket elects one answerer, the digest goes through MLS - and nothing orders them, so the answerer waits three seconds for the digest before falling back to sending its whole store. That fallback is not a safety net but the rollout story: a device too old to send a digest gets exactly the behaviour it expects, and a new answerer facing an old asker degrades to the previous protocol without any negotiation
- **A conversation that is missing messages now stops asking for them when it actually has them, and asks a different way when asking is not working.** Two things followed from the diff above. First, a device recorded as "still missing history" used to be cleared by the mere ARRIVAL of a bundle - but a long history is sent in chunks, so the first chunk ended the wait while the rest was still coming, and a peer that had listed forty messages we lack was considered answered by forty unrelated ones. What ends the wait is now the evidence, not the event: an EMPTY bundle is the only authoritative "you are missing nothing" (nobody sends one without having compared their whole store, and nobody sends one while short themselves), while a bundle full of messages voids a presumption - "I hold nothing for this conversation" stops being true - and leaves a proof standing. So the wait empties itself: each exchange strictly reduces the difference and converges on that empty bundle, instead of stopping at whichever chunk happened to arrive first. Second, the repair used when a message cannot be decrypted asks the sender for "everything you sent in the last two minutes", read out of memory - which fails outright if the sender reloaded, if the message aged out, or if the loss is older than the window reaches, and no amount of repeating it helps. The client now asks for the difference straight away, alongside that request rather than after it - the difference reads the peer's saved messages rather than its memory, is answered by one elected device rather than all of them, and names messages by id rather than by time. While it was there, the sender's retention stopped being a hand-picked five minutes and became what it always meant: the widest window a peer can ask about, plus a round trip. Three of those five minutes could never be requested by anyone and only kept plaintext in memory for longer
- **The repair that pools history between your devices now asks once per event instead of on two independent schedules.** Two retry ladders, in two different files, drove the same request, so the traffic one conversation could generate was their product and no single place could be read to predict it - and one of them left an entry behind that silenced every later attempt for the lifetime of the tab, so a repair could go permanently quiet exactly when it was needed. There is now one request per occasion, ignored outright while one is already outstanding, re-attempted when something actually changes (a reconnection, a device coming back online, a newly detected loss) and stopped by a proof rather than a budget: a device that compared its whole store and found nothing missing. Nine durations decided this mechanism; one remains, and its only job is to notice that an attempt went unanswered
- **Revoking a device now lapses after ten years instead of never.** Deleting a device from the security page records its identifier so the same physical device cannot come back under it - the identifier is deliberately stable across a reinstall, which is what makes the record necessary. That record was kept for ever, so a table that only ever grew, holding identifiers retired a decade earlier whose hardware is long gone. It now expires, and the expiry is applied where the question "is this device banned" is actually asked, so a lapsed record stops banning immediately whether or not the nightly cleanup has run yet - the cleanup only reclaims the space. Revoking a device again restarts its ten years rather than keeping the first date, so a deliberate second revocation can never be born already expired
- **Media storage moved from MinIO to Garage** (MinIO is no longer maintained upstream). `media-service` talks to it through the exact same generic S3 client as before - Garage implements every S3 operation the code calls, so `storage.service.ts` did not change, and every env var keeps its `MINIO_*` name on purpose. Garage self-provisions its bucket from a dedicated key (`GARAGE_ACCESS_KEY_ID`/`SECRET_ACCESS_KEY`) rather than reusing MinIO's own credentials - Garage enforces a minimum key length MinIO never did, and this deployment's MinIO credentials were shorter than that, which crashed the container on its first prod boot until it was given its own key. Every existing object was copied across with `rclone sync` (Garage's on-disk format isn't MinIO's, so this went through the S3 protocol rather than a volume copy) and verified with `rclone check` - 0 diffs, 200 objects / 45.370 MiB matching on both sides - before `media-service` was repointed. The old `minio` service is removed; its volume is kept for a two-week rollback window rather than deleted outright. Full mechanism and the exact differences (health check, credential bootstrap, two data volumes instead of one) are on [docker](docs/wiki/infrastructure/docker.md)
- **The nightly backup no longer copies every stored file all over again, and a restore now refuses to finish having quietly skipped them.** Photos, videos and documents are encrypted on the device before they are uploaded, which means the server holds bytes that cannot be compressed and never change once written - and the backup was re-archiving all of them every night and keeping fifteen nights, so every byte anyone stored cost sixteen on a disk that has a fixed size. The projection for a few hundred daily users had the disk filling within weeks, and the files themselves were not the problem: the copies were. They are now backed up into a deduplicating store, where a night in which nothing changed costs almost nothing - the second run measured twenty-four kilobytes - and the same history fits in the space of a single copy plus whatever is new. It was proven before being trusted, by restoring the whole store into a scratch directory and comparing every file with the live one: a hundred and seventy-two files, identical byte for byte. The fifteen existing archives were kept, not deleted, because they also carry the only backup of the databases; only the file-store portion was removed from each, taking the whole set from just over a gigabyte to a hundred and thirty-three megabytes on both the server and its off-site mirror. The restore script now takes its files from the new store automatically, and if it can find them in neither place it stops with an explanation instead of reporting a successful restore that was missing the great majority of the data - the old code would have skipped them without a word. The backup's own manifest was also corrected: it claimed the message history lived in a database that is, in fact, completely empty and that no part of the application connects to. That history is in PostgreSQL and always was, so it was being backed up correctly the whole time - but anyone reading the manifest during an incident would have believed otherwise
- **The in-memory cache the server uses for presence and repairs can no longer grow until the machine runs out.** It was running with no ceiling and a policy of refusing new writes once memory ran out - which, for a component every message path touches, turns a slow leak into a service that stops accepting anything. It now has a one-gigabyte ceiling and, on reaching it, drops the oldest entries that were already set to expire on their own. That is strictly safer than what was there: nothing without an expiry date is ever dropped, and once the expiring entries are gone the behaviour is exactly what it was before. It is not a budget - the measured usage is 2.29 MB, four hundred times under the cap - it is a ceiling so an unnoticed leak degrades instead of taking the server down.
- **When a photo is uploaded without being shrunk, the app now says which of the seven reasons applied - and the plan to shrink them harder was dropped, because measuring it showed there was nothing there.** The storage plan assumed the multi-megabyte files on the server were phone photos going up untouched, and that re-encoding them would divide the total by five or ten. Both halves are wrong. Every upload path already compresses, and measuring the app's own compressor over twelve nine-megapixel photographs put the average result at **245 KB** - while the five largest files on the server are 4.15 to 7.86 MB, sixteen to thirty-two times that. No image that went through the compressor can be one of those, so lowering the quality would only shrink the files that were never the problem, at a real cost in sharpness; it was not done, and the measurements are recorded so the question does not have to be re-argued from taste. What can produce a file that size is a **video**, which is not compressed at all, or one of the compressor's give-up paths - the most expensive being a photo in a format this device cannot decode, typically **HEIC from an iPhone**, which was handed back at full size in complete silence. All of them now log what they did and how big the file was, so the next large upload can be traced from the client log instead of being an unexplained object on disk.
- **The media server now reports a retention delete that failed instead of ignoring it.** It was ignored on the grounds that the file might simply be gone already - but this is precisely how a file gets stranded: the entry is marked deleted anyway, the entry itself is eventually cleaned up, and the daily sweep only ever looks at entries, so a file whose deletion failed becomes invisible to it permanently. Seven of the twenty-six files currently on the server have no entry at all, the two largest among them.
- **A salon notification no longer carries three pieces of information nothing has ever read.** Every push for a community message had been sending the community's internal identifier, the message's identifier and the time it was written, to every device, for the whole life of the feature - and not one of the three programs that receive them looked at any of it. They are gone. Two of them could not have been used as they were: the community identifier is a number, not a name, and no notification can turn one into the other, so putting the community's name in the title needs the NAME sent - a separate change, and a decision about the wording, still to be made. The other two belong to a mechanism the private conversations have and salons deliberately do not, since a salon's history is kept by the server and re-read rather than stored on the device. What is left is exactly what a device reads, which also leaves a little more room under the size limit these notifications share with the message text itself. And the contract between the one program that sends and the three that read is now checked automatically, in both directions - it had been checked by nothing at all, which is how three fields drifted into being sent to nobody and a fourth into being read by nobody
- **A new automatic check refuses any sentence written into the program that a translation table already carries, on both platforms.** The check that shipped a day earlier held the translation files against each other, which cannot see a sentence that never entered a file at all - that is exactly how the six above survived. This one asks a different question: does this piece of text already exist as something we translate? Three things had to be true for it to catch what it was written for, and each was measured rather than assumed. It compares with accents and escapes ignored, because the defect was spelled `Repondre` without its accent and `Appel vid\u{00e9}o entrant` with the accent written as a code; it ignores decoration at either end, because two of the six were an emoji followed by a sentence the table does carry; and it compares against the FRENCH side only, because every internal name in these programs is English and four of them collide with an English translation - so French is the one language in which such a piece of text can only ever be something a user reads. It was verified by putting all six back and watching it name them, file and line, rather than by trusting that it would

### Fixed
- **On the phone and the desktop app, three requests to the server never left the device - and one of them destroyed data because of it.** In a browser the app is served from the same address as the server, so asking for a page of the server's own by its short name works. Inside the mobile and desktop app it is served from the device itself, so the same short name asks the *device* for that page - which does not have it, and answers with the app's own start page instead, reporting complete success. The request never reached the server, and nothing looked wrong: no error, no failure, a perfectly normal "it worked". Two of the three merely made a feature unavailable - the "first PIN" wording could no longer tell a brand-new account from an existing one, and paying for an event ticket could not start. The third is the reason this is written down: "I forgot my PIN" asks the server to clear the PIN on the account, then wipes the encryption state on the device so a new PIN can be chosen from scratch. It was reading that false success, so it wiped the device - and the account's PIN had never actually been cleared, leaving the person with their old encrypted history gone and a new PIN the server would keep rejecting. All three now name the server explicitly, the way every other call in the app already did, and a test refuses any future request written the short way - the point being not that three call sites were wrong, but that this particular mistake announces itself as success
- **On Android, the app could permanently lose the credential it uses to work in the background - and it happened at a reboot, before you had even unlocked the phone.** Found from a remark about a notification after a restart: the sender's photo was missing. It was the visible tip of the whole background credential being rejected by the server, for the entire life of that process. The cause is a chain worth stating in full, because no part of it is obviously wrong on its own. After a restart, Android creates the app's process **before your first unlock** - not by our doing: the notification library we use registers a component that asks to run at that moment, and one such component is enough to start everything else with it. In that state the phone's encrypted storage is not open yet, so files report themselves as absent rather than unreadable, and the key store answers "cannot read" for a key that is perfectly intact. The startup code then did the one thing it must never do: it took "cannot read" for "corrupt", **deleted the key and made a new one**. Everything that key protected became unreadable for good, and the app went on serving notifications with a credential the server refuses - a missing photo where you can see it, and, on the same credential, the fallback that fetches a message the notification did not carry. The app now knows the difference between "not there" and "not readable yet": it does none of this startup work while the phone is still locked, does all of it the moment you unlock, and a key it cannot read while locked is never destroyed. A rejected credential is also logged as the authentication failure it is, instead of passing for an ordinary missing avatar.
- **On the phone and the desktop app, saving a message to the device could fail outright while several conversations were catching up at once - and a message that fails to save is one you no longer have.** The app stores your messages in a small database on the device, and it was writing a batch of them the way one normally does: open a transaction, write, close it. The library underneath, however, keeps a handful of separate connections to that database and picks whichever is free for each instruction - so "open" could go to one connection, the writing to a second, and "close" to a third. The transaction nobody closed then stayed open for the rest of the session, and every later attempt to write was refused with "database is locked". Caught on a real device on 11 August, where it failed the save of a message that had just been sent and the queueing of another; the same run also proved the cause directly, by asking the database to open two transactions at once and watching both succeed, which is only possible with more than one connection. Batches are now written as a single instruction, which the database treats as a unit on its own - no opening or closing needed, and nothing left open to block anyone. Very large batches are split into several instructions, which is safe here because each message is written under its own identifier, so writing it twice changes nothing
- **A phone silently threw away messages it could not decrypt, and then told other devices their conversation was complete.** Found by re-running the deliberate-break test above and watching it still lose six messages, this time for a completely different reason. When a device asks its peers for the messages it is missing, the server picks one of their devices at random to answer - a phone as readily as a browser, which is correct, and there is a rule that a device still missing history itself must stay silent rather than certify anyone. The phone answered "we hold the same thing, nothing to send", ended the repair, and it held none of the six. It was not lying: it had never noticed losing them. The shared Rust core treats "the key for this message was already used" as an ordinary duplicate delivery and reports nothing at all - which is right when the same message really does arrive twice, and wrong when a sender whose state went backwards encrypts a NEW message with an already-used key, because the two are only distinguishable by comparing the message against the ones already seen, which happens a layer above. On the web that layer found out anyway, by reading the log line the core prints on its way past; on a phone that log goes to the system console where nothing reads it, so the phone learnt nothing, recorded no gap, and was therefore considered a trustworthy witness. The core now reports it and lets the layer that can tell them apart decide. Both pieces of native code that were supposed to handle this had been written correctly, with comments explaining exactly this case - and neither had ever run, because the error never got past the core to reach them. The same swallow was left in place on the bulk history-replay path at the time, on the reasoning that an already-seen message genuinely is the expected case there - it is now gone too, from both the phone and the browser, because "expected" is a statement about frequency and the two cases still need telling apart: the replay compares each message against the ones it has already read, so a message arriving at a spent key that this device has never read is the same lost message, and it now records the gap instead of dropping it silently
- **A conversation broken by a small rewind lost messages for good, while a badly broken one repaired itself completely.** Found by deliberately breaking a real conversation between two browsers on production - restoring an older copy of one client's encryption state, which is exactly the accident the whole loss investigation has been chasing, done on purpose and to a chosen depth. Twelve messages' worth of rewind: five messages sent afterwards never reached the other side and never will, the sender showing all of them and the receiver none. Sixty messages' worth: everything arrived, sixteen out of sixteen. The deeper break healing where the shallower one did not is the finding, and the reason is the order of the two repairs. When a message cannot be decrypted the receiver first asks the sender to send again, and only after three such requests in five minutes does it reach for the exchange that compares saved histories. But that first request is only ever triggered by one situation - a sender whose encryption state went backwards - and it asks that sender to encrypt again with the very state that is wrong, so the resend collides exactly as the original did. It was observed re-sending one, then five, fifteen and twenty-five messages, and delivering none of them. What it does accomplish is to burn through the overlap: five real messages plus seven resent ones is twelve, precisely the depth of the shallow break, which is why that conversation appeared to recover on its own - it had run out of collision, not been repaired, and the messages caught in the overlap were already gone. Meanwhile three requests spaced thirty seconds apart need over a minute of continuous loss before the exchange that actually works is even attempted, and a small break is over long before that. So the ladder was gated on failures of a rung that could not succeed. The comparison of saved histories now starts at the FIRST lost message, alongside the resend request instead of behind it. The resend is kept - it costs a single control message and does win the narrow case where the sender has already moved past the collision by the time the request lands - but nothing waits on it
- **A message could be lost without leaving a single trace, and the app had no way of noticing.** Messages carry the version of the conversation they were encrypted in, and a device keeps the keys to the two previous versions - so a message that arrives just after somebody was added to the conversation still reads normally. Beyond that the keys are gone, which is exactly the situation of a device that has re-joined a conversation: it holds nothing from before it re-joined. Such a message was being treated as if it were an internal housekeeping frame - those legitimately arrive late, have no content, and must be discarded in silence - so a real message somebody sent was discarded in that same silence: no warning, nothing recorded, no repair attempted, and it was acknowledged to the server, which then had no reason to keep it. It was found on the production server on 11 August, where the only thing it left behind was a log line that ordinary housekeeping also writes. The two cases are now told apart by a field the message carries in the clear, so no decryption is needed to read it, and a real message reaching this state is reported as lost and repaired the same way as any other loss: the device asks the others what it is missing, by name, and they re-send it. Nothing changes for the housekeeping frames, and nothing changes for the ordinary case of a message overtaken by someone joining - that still reads normally, which is why the check is on the message's type rather than simply on its age
- **Having messages waiting for you could stop the app from ever connecting - and the more it mattered, the more certain it was.** When the app starts it reads each conversation back from the server's shared copy, and before doing so it makes sure nothing is still waiting for this device on the delivery queue, because a message must never be taken in by both routes at once. That check does not only wait, it also collects: if nothing has fetched the queue yet, it fetches it. What it collected was then handed to the part of the app that opens messages - which is set up later in the same startup, a fraction of a second afterwards. So the check fetched messages into a queue that had nothing to empty it, and then waited for that queue to empty. It never could. Startup stopped there, before the app had even worked out which tab owns the connection, so no connection was opened at all: no messages in, no messages out, no error on screen, and nothing that would recover except closing the app and hoping the queue had been cleared some other way - which nothing else could do either, so every subsequent start stopped in the same place. The condition for hitting it was simply having anything waiting on the server at the moment you opened the app, which is the ordinary situation of anyone who has been away. Measured on production: a browser holding two waiting messages stopped dead on every reload, while another running the identical code with an empty queue started normally - the backlog alone decided it. The part that opens messages is now set up before anything can fetch them, so what is collected is taken in immediately, as it always did once the app was running. The check itself also refuses to run at all if it is ever asked before that part exists: it says so in the log and lets the caller continue, because reading a conversation slightly too early is recoverable and an app that never connects again is not
- **An outage lasting more than a few minutes left every open tab dead until it was reloaded, and said nothing.** When the connection drops the app retries, waiting a little longer between each attempt up to half a minute. After twenty failures it gave up entirely and set a flag that refused every later attempt - permanently, until one of two things happened: the app coming back to the foreground, or the device changing network. Neither of those can happen to a tab that is already in the foreground on a network that has not changed, which is the ordinary situation of a desktop browser left open. So a browser tab that was open during a server outage stayed disconnected for ever after the server came back. Measured on two production tabs seven hours after such an outage: the component whose job is to notice a dead connection was still doing so every minute, and its request to reconnect was being discarded in silence each time, with not one connection attempted in over two minutes of watching. The badge read "Hors-ligne" and was telling the truth; nothing else said anything at all. Giving up is now impossible: the only things that stop the app retrying are being logged out and the server answering that the session is over, both of which are answers rather than failures. Retrying costs one attempt every thirty seconds, far less than the connection it is trying to restore. Returning to the foreground and regaining a network still matter - they restart the waiting from the beginning, so recovery is immediate rather than up to half a minute later - but nothing depends on them any more. Two related faults were found while fixing this one: after a failed attempt, the app's own instruction to schedule the next one was being discarded (it was issued a fraction too early, while the previous attempt still counted as running), so the real retry driver was never the waiting ladder but the once-a-minute check beside it; and when a reconnection was forced while an attempt was already scheduled, the scheduled one was abandoned rather than cancelled and went on running in parallel
- **On a phone, one failed reconnection could leave the app disconnected for good, showing "En attente de connexion" while nothing was connecting.** Found from the user asking why the banner was up. The phone was sitting on a chat screen with a working internet connection - it was fetching over HTTP the whole time - and had not attempted a single reconnection in the twenty minutes that could be read back from the system log; cutting the wifi made it reconnect in a third of a second, which is what showed the network had never been the problem. Two causes, and they defeated each other. Going into the background stops the timers that watch the connection, correctly, because the system is about to freeze the app - but nothing started them again on the way back, they being armed only once when you sign in. So after a single trip to the background and back, a phone had nothing left able to notice a socket that died later. Meanwhile the reconnection itself gives up after twenty failed attempts and latches, and nothing could unlatch it: a successful connection resets the attempt count but not the latch, and the timer whose job is to notice a dead connection asks the very function the latch turns off - a safety cut-out that also cut the wire to the thing that would have reset it. Returning to the foreground now resets the latch, restarts both timers and only then reconnects, which also means it runs when the connection survived the background, since the timers were stopped either way. The message logged when it gives up used to tell you to click "Retry"; no such button has ever existed anywhere in the app, so it now says what actually recovers it
- **A conversation that failed to catch up on its history because nobody was online stopped asking for the rest of the session.** The situation the retries exist for was the exact situation that disabled them. A solicitation registers itself while its attempts are scheduled, and every other trigger - reconnecting, a peer coming back, giving up on the narrow repair - skips a group that is already asking, which is right. But nothing ever removed that registration when the attempts simply ran out unanswered: it is cleared by a bundle that ends the wait, or by a fresh solicitation, and neither happens when no peer was ever reachable. So a group whose members were all offline during its three-minute burst was marked "already asking" for the life of the tab and never asked again - a page reload being the only cure, on precisely the conversations that needed it most. The end of a burst is now read from the burst's own schedule, which is known when it starts, rather than from the presence of a registration. Alongside it, the session gained a slow sweep - every fifteen minutes, paused while the tab is hidden, and doing nothing at all unless some conversation is recorded as short - because every other trigger is an event a long-lived session is not guaranteed to see again: presence, in particular, is only polled for people the interface is actually displaying, so a peer returning goes unnoticed for any conversation not on screen
- **A device with a big enough backlog could never collect it, and the backlog only grew.** Messages waiting for a device that was away are fetched in batches, and the size of a batch was counted in messages - five hundred at a time. A message carrying a photo is thousands of times larger than one carrying a word, so on a phone that had been away from a conversation full of media, five hundred messages meant twelve megabytes in a single response. The app gives each batch ten seconds to arrive, that one never did, and a batch that does not arrive is not confirmed to the server - so nothing was removed, the next attempt asked for the same five hundred messages, and met the same twelve megabytes. A closed loop: measured on 13 August on a phone whose queue had reached nine hundred and seventy-six messages and thirty-six megabytes, rising every hour for weeks and never once falling. Two things changed, either of which is enough on its own. The server now fills a batch up to a size rather than a count, so a response is about a megabyte whatever the messages contain - and always contains at least one message, so a single very large one is still delivered instead of blocking its own queue for ever. And the app, when a batch fails to arrive, halves what it asks for and asks again rather than giving up: nine steps at most, down to a single message, which is the smallest question there is. That second half is what protects a phone whose connection is simply too slow for any fixed size, and what would have contained this fault years earlier. The report that watches for a device falling behind was also part of why nobody noticed: it warned above two thousand *messages*, a threshold set after an unrelated incident, so nine hundred and seventy-six messages weighing thirty-six megabytes never tripped it. It now watches size as well as count, and lists the worst offenders by size
- **Collecting a backlog stopped after the first batch, and a message could be skipped for good.** Two faults in the same place, both found by watching the fix above actually run on production rather than by reading it. The app decided it had collected everything when a batch came back smaller than it had asked for - which was true while a batch was counted in messages, and stopped being true the moment batches were measured in size instead: the server answered a request for five hundred messages with the fifty-three that fit in a megabyte, and the app read that as "there is nothing else" and stopped, with eight hundred and seventy still waiting. It would have taken one reconnection per batch. Only an empty answer proves there is nothing left, so that is now the only thing that ends the collection. The second is rarer and worse: the app resumes from the moment of the last message it received, and messages are timed to the millisecond, so two messages queued in the same millisecond could straddle the boundary between two batches - and the second one would then be skipped by every later request, permanently undelivered while still sitting on the server. One such pair was present in the live queue the day this shipped. A batch now always carries a whole group of messages sharing a moment, even when that takes it slightly over its size limit; a message is never worth losing to keep a batch tidy
- **A burst of notifications could get the Android app killed by the system, after which nothing arrived at all.** Each incoming push was handled on a thread of its own. That is fine for one message and gets worse the more there are, because all of them then compete for the single lock that guards the encryption state: every thread waits its turn for up to five seconds, each one that gets in re-reads the whole state from storage, and each one that gives up tries again. Behind a backlog - a phone that was off, or out of signal for a while - that meant dozens of threads doing almost nothing but waiting, and Android eventually shut the app down for using too much processor in the background. A closed app delivers no notifications and sends none of your pending messages, so the real cost was the app going quiet, not the battery. Everything touching the encryption state now runs one after another on a single worker: the same work in the same order, without the crowd. Making them wait adds no delay, because the lock had already made them wait - it only removes the fighting over it
- **The app froze - sometimes to the point of Android offering to close it - after a Play Store update, and the file holding your identity and your conversations was twice the size it needed to be.** Both come from the same detail. That file is written and read as a series of buffers of raw bytes, but the encoder had never been told they were bytes, so it wrote each one as a list of individual numbers and read it back one number at a time, re-parsing a header for every single byte. On a 2.7 MB file that is millions of parses. Measured here on a desktop: reading the file the old way took 21.6 ms, the new way 0.5 ms - forty-five times faster - and the file itself is exactly half the size. What turned a slow read into a freeze was that a phone catching up on a backlog of unsent messages reloaded and rewrote that entire file **once per message**, so ten queued messages meant ten full reads and ten full writes, all of it inside the one minute Android allows a background task before it kills it. A catch-up now loads the file once, encrypts every message against it, and saves once. It also takes a bounded number of messages at a time and picks the rest up on the next pass, so a large backlog is delivered over several passes rather than risking being cut off in the middle of one. Existing installs are read exactly as before and converted the first time they save; nothing is lost and nothing needs doing.
- **A message the app could not yet handle made it re-download the whole backlog every fifteen seconds, for the rest of the session.** Some messages cannot be dealt with the moment they arrive - one for a conversation the app has not finished loading, or for a group it has not yet been let into - so they are deliberately left on the server to be fetched again. "Again" was a timer: every fifteen seconds, fetch the backlog, fail on the same messages, and put the sync overlay back up while doing it. On a device whose group never came back that ran until the app was closed, which is what the permanent "synchronisation" overlay was. Both reasons a message gets left behind have an event that actually resolves them - being let into the group, or the conversation finishing loading - so the app now waits for that event instead of for a clock. No event, no re-fetch
- **A conversation could start broadcasting to itself for as long as a tab stayed open, with nobody typing - and the phone sat behind the "Synchronisation des messages" banner while it caught up.** Measured on production on 7 August 2026: three clients held one DM at roughly 430 technical messages per minute for thirteen minutes, 4 921 of them queued for a single phone, which then spent eighteen minutes draining them. Not a word had been typed. It starts from a legitimate repair - when a device reports it could not decrypt a message, the sender is the only party that can still produce it, so it sends it again - and that repair is meant to die out after five minutes of quiet. It did not: every replay was recorded as though it were a fresh send, which pushed the expiry out indefinitely and stopped the payload being recognised as one already held. The retained set therefore never emptied, and each later report replayed all of it. A replay is no longer counted as a send, so the repair is bounded again: with no real activity, it decays to nothing within five minutes.
- **An iPhone had never received a single notification from a community.** Not a salon message, not a post, not a form reminder - nothing that comes from the community side of Canari has ever appeared on an iPhone, for the whole life of those features, while the same message notified Android correctly and the server recorded it as sent. The cause is worth stating plainly because it is not exotic: there were two copies of the code that hands a notification to Google's delivery service, and only one of them had been kept up to date. The maintained copy attaches a small block of instructions that Apple requires - what kind of notification this is, whether it may be shown, whether the phone should wake up and decrypt it. The forgotten copy attached nothing, and a notification with nothing attached is, to an iPhone, a message with no instructions: silently discarded. Everything downstream was correct and well tested - the instructions themselves had tests covering every field - and none of that applied, because the part of the program that actually sends was not the part being tested. There is now one copy, and the test looks at the message as it leaves the process rather than at the recipe it was supposed to follow
- **Notifications for community channels had never once been sent, and a check meant to protect invitations was answering "yes" without looking.** Each of Canari's back-end services publishes its routes under a common `/api` prefix, and the addresses they use to call each other are configured without it - so every caller has to add it. Three calls did not. Two of them are the ones that ask the messaging service to send a push notification, which means writing in a community channel notified nobody, ever, on any device: the request was answered "no such address", the answer was written to a log as a warning, and the message was delivered normally, so the only visible symptom was a notification that never came. The third asks whether a user has any device able to receive encrypted messages, before inviting them into a conversation - and it is written to answer "yes" when it cannot reach the service, so that a momentary outage does not block an invitation. Against an address that does not exist, that safe default was the only answer it ever gave: not a weakened check, but no check at all. The same mistake was in the account service, where it left an account deletion incomplete - the encryption keys, devices and stored messages on one side, the posts, follows and memberships on the other, all asked to be erased at an address that answered nothing, each failure logged as a warning and none of them retried. Every one of these is written as best-effort, tolerating a failure on the assumption that it is temporary; here the failure was permanent, which is exactly the case that assumption cannot survive. The prefix is no longer any caller's to remember: each service now builds the address through a single function that inserts it once, and the four calls that were already correct go through it too, so the next one added cannot get it wrong. Found by reading the servers' own logs during a client test run, which is the only place these had ever been visible
- **Deleting a message you had written but not yet sent SENT it, and then took it back.** Write a message with no network, change your mind and delete it before the connection returns: the moment it came back, the other person received the text, saw it appear, and only then received the instruction to remove it. You deleted something that had never left your phone, and it was sent anyway. The cause was that both halves were queued the same way, side by side, so the queue delivered them in order the moment it could. A message still waiting in the queue is now WITHDRAWN from it instead - nothing is sent, and there is nothing to take back. The instruction to delete only travels when the message really has left the device, which is what it was always for. Deleting a message that had already been delivered is unchanged
- **Deleting a message you had not yet sent left a "message deleted" line behind on your own device, for ever.** Since messages waiting to be sent are now withdrawn rather than sent and taken back, nobody else ever receives them - but your own conversation still showed the grey placeholder that means "something was here and was deleted", standing for a message that had never existed anywhere but on that one device. Nothing could ever clear it: the other people have no such message to remove, and the server never held one. It is now removed outright, from the screen and from the device's stored copy, while a message that really had been delivered keeps its placeholder exactly as before - there, the placeholder stands for something the others genuinely received. It was found by the tool that compares two devices' conversations message by message, which read those leftovers as four messages one device had and the others had lost - the same shape a real loss has
- **A deleted message could come back readable on a device that rebuilt the conversation from another one.** The copy a device sends when another is missing history says which messages were deleted, and the receiving device recorded that fact - but kept the original text alongside it and wrote that text back to its own storage. On screen it showed as deleted, so nothing looked wrong; the deleted words were simply still there underneath, and worse, that device would then pass them on to the next one asking for history. The text is now replaced when the deletion is recorded, so what remains of a deleted message is the marker and nothing else
- **Reacting to a message you had deleted brought it back, and reading an edited one undid the edit.** Everything that can happen to a message - a reaction, an edit, a deletion, a read receipt - was saved to the device the same way: rebuild the whole message from what this particular piece of code happened to know about it, then write it over the stored one. None of them knew everything. The code handling reactions knew nothing about edits or deletions, the one handling read receipts knew nothing about either, the one handling deletions knew nothing about edits - so each of them, in saving what it did know, quietly erased what it did not. The message on screen stayed correct, because that copy was being changed properly; only the saved one was damaged, so nothing was visible until the next time the app was opened, at which point a deleted message could be readable again, an edited one back to its original wording, and a message you had read unread. Which fields survived depended on which of these was the last to touch that message, which is why it looked random. There is now a single way to save a change to a message: say what changed, and everything else is left exactly as it was found. Removing something is still possible, but only by asking for it - which is what an edit does when it marks its new wording as read by nobody
- **Reactions, edits, deletions and read receipts had no copy anywhere except on the devices that happened to be awake when they were sent.** The server keeps a copy of a conversation - as ciphertext it cannot read - so that a device coming back after a while can catch up on its own rather than having to find another of your devices online at that exact moment. That copy was only ever given the messages themselves. Everything that *changes* a message was left out, and not by a decision: those are sent with a flag meaning "do not show a notification for this", and the server was using that same flag to decide what was worth keeping. So a reaction was silent, and being silent it was also forgotten. The consequence was invisible until a device was away long enough: it came back, received the messages, and none of the corrections applied to them - a message you had deleted came back readable, an edit was missing, things you had read were unread again - and there was no way for it to find out, because the only remaining copy was on a device that might be switched off. The two meanings have been separated: whether something notifies you and whether it is worth keeping are now decided independently, and everything that changes a conversation is kept. The reverse mistake is prevented too - the server records, for each thing it keeps, whether it was meant to be visible, so a device rejoining is never notified about a reaction from last week
- **Messages you had read came back unread after closing the app, and catching up on history could mark old messages unread all over again.** What each person had read was recorded message by message, as a list of names attached to each one - which only works for messages the device actually holds, and only if the record survives. Neither held. Your own reading was applied on screen and never written down, so it was correct until you closed the app and gone when you reopened it: everything you had read came back unread, and stayed that way until somebody else's device happened to send back a copy saying you had read it. And because a message can only carry that list once it exists on the device, receiving an older message later - a catch-up, a new phone rebuilding a conversation - produced a message nobody had read, since the acknowledgement for it had been sent long before it arrived. What is recorded now is one point per person: *read up to here*. It covers messages the device has not received yet, so a catch-up cannot un-read anything; it is kept with the conversation rather than with each message, so recording that a thousand messages were read is one small write instead of a thousand; and it travels in the copy the server keeps, so a reinstall recovers where you were without needing another of your devices to be awake. Two visible consequences, both deliberate: it is written down the moment you read, so it survives closing the app; and editing a message no longer makes it unread again for everyone - that could never have been agreed on anyway, since anyone who did not receive the edit would go on considering it read
- **Everything you had read came back unread the next time the app was opened - one layer further down than the last fix of the same name.** Where each person had read up to is now recorded on the conversation and written to the device the moment you read, which is what the previous entry describes. It was written correctly and never read back: when the app starts it rebuilds each conversation from what is stored, and that rebuild copied the name, the state and the date of the last message, but not the read marks - so every conversation started the session believing nobody had read anything. The badge then counted messages you had already read on another device, and the marker under your own messages had to wait for somebody else's device to hand the information back. The rebuild now carries every field the save writes. A value written but never read back is worse than one that was never stored at all: the write succeeds, so nothing anywhere reports a problem
- **A pinned message did not survive on a new device.** Pin something in a conversation, then sign in on a new phone or a new browser: everything came back except the pin. Pins in a community channel did come back, which is what made this a gap rather than a decision - the two behaved differently for no reason a user could see. A conversation's pins now travel with its history, both as the pin and unpin actions themselves and as the finished list, because the action that pinned a message can be older than the window a server keeps while the pin is not. Pinning and unpinning now also record WHEN, the way a reaction always has, so two devices that disagree settle it on the later of the two instead of on whichever answer happened to arrive last - which is what stops someone who has not yet heard about an unpin from bringing the pin back
- **Taking a reaction back often did not reach the other people in the conversation.** Removing a reaction was sent as an instruction to forget it, and forgetting works only if it arrives - so a device that was off, or that later rebuilt the conversation from someone else's copy, ended up with the reaction back. Worse, the repair itself could not correct it: when two devices compared what they held, a device took the other's reactions only if it had none of its own, so anyone holding the reaction you had removed kept it for good, and the two never agreed again. A device that had hit the fifteen-emoji limit also silently refused reactions everyone else accepted, which left it permanently different for a reason nobody could see. Reactions are now handled as a record per person and per emoji, each carrying the moment it last changed and whether it currently stands, and the more recent one always wins. Removing a reaction is now the same message that placed it, sent the other way round, so it travels exactly as reliably. Two devices that have seen the same events end up identical whatever order those events arrived in, and seeing one twice changes nothing. The limit still applies to what you can place, and no longer to what the app is willing to accept from others
- **Messages restored from another device could come back in a different order each time the app was opened.** Two messages sent in the same millisecond are separated by the moment the server accepted them, which is why that second time is stored alongside each message. When a device rebuilt a conversation from another one's copy, it kept that second time on one of the two routes the copy can take and dropped it on the other - so whether the order was stable depended on how the conversation had been recovered. Both routes now keep it
- **A device that rebuilt a conversation from someone else's copy showed "modifie" with no date, for ever.** When a device is missing history it asks another one, which sends back what it holds. That copy said whether each message had been edited but not when, and there is no second source for it: the app never receives an echo of an edit it made itself, and the other people's edits arrive only as they happen. So a message restored this way carried the marker permanently without a date, and nothing could ever fill it in. The time now travels with the flag
- **A conversation could open completely empty - the name, the photo and the message box, and not one message - while every message was still safely on the device.** You could get out of it by opening another conversation and coming back, which is exactly what makes it so misleading: it looks like the messages are gone, or like the network is down, and it is neither. The chat does not draw a whole conversation at once, it draws a window onto the last screenful and widens it as you scroll up; the position of that window was worked out when you opened the conversation and then never checked again. Meanwhile, opening a conversation also refreshes it, and that refresh replaces what is loaded with the most recent sixty messages. So a conversation that had a long history loaded - after catching up, or after receiving a batch of older messages from another device - opened with its window pointing far down that long list, the list was replaced by a much shorter one a moment later, and the window ended up pointing past the end of it: nothing to draw. Leaving and coming back recomputed the position against the shorter list, which is why the workaround worked. Seen on production with 598 messages stored and none on screen, surviving a full page reload. The window is now checked against the conversation as it actually is every time it is drawn, so a conversation that has messages always shows some.
- **A message that arrived while a conversation was still loading appeared on screen and then vanished.** Opening a conversation starts a catch-up: the app fetches whatever is new, decrypts it, saves it, and then re-reads a page from its local store to display. That last step took a few seconds, and anything delivered in the meantime was shown as it arrived and then wiped out when the catch-up finished - because the page had been read before that message existed, and the app put it on screen in place of everything, rather than alongside what was already there. The message was never lost: it stayed in the sidebar preview as the latest one, and reopening the conversation brought it back. But between the two, it was simply gone from the thread you were looking at, with nothing to say so. Measured on a live conversation: shown at half a second, gone at three and a half. The same thing happened in community channels, where the wait is longer still, and it also made the unread count come out one short. A freshly read page is now merged with what is on screen instead of replacing it - it decides only the stretch of the conversation it actually covers, so newer arrivals, older messages you had scrolled back to, and anything still being sent all stay put
- **A photo deleted by the 30-day retention showed up as a broken image everywhere except in the conversation itself - and under a post it displayed the internal error code.** Media that nobody opens for thirty days are deleted from the server; that is deliberate, and it is the reason photos do not grow without bound. The conversation has said so plainly since June - "Média expiré (rétention 30 jours)" - but it was the only place that did. Under a post, the same media printed `MEDIA_PURGED_BY_RETENTION` in red, as an error, which is an internal token no reader can act on. In the shared-media grid of a conversation it was a generic broken-image icon, indistinguishable from a picture that failed to load because the network hiccuped. Opening one of those from that grid was the worst of the four: the viewer opened on a spinner and spun for ever, waiting for a file the server will never send. All four now say the same thing, and none of them says it in red - an expired photo is not a failure, it is the end of its life, and the difference decides whether you retry, blame your connection, or ask the sender to post it again. The underlying cause was that "expired" and "failed" were told apart by searching the error text for a magic word, so only the one place that happened to do the search behaved correctly; the distinction is now carried by the error itself and every surface reads it the same way. Trying to download an expired file from that panel also used to do nothing at all, silently - it now tells you why.
- **The rule that keeps a file alive while people are still opening it had never actually been running in production.** The app tells the server when it opens a photo, video or document it already had a copy of, so the thirty-day deletion clock follows real use rather than the one download that first fetched it. The server half of that shipped on 11 August and the deployment that carried it failed - not on its own account, but because the *frontend* image being built alongside it failed, and one image's failure cancelled every other image in the same batch. The media service's own build had been fine. Nothing revisited it afterwards either, because each deployment only rebuilds what its own changes touched, and no later change touched that service: the endpoint answered "no such address" in production for a day, every report from every app was rejected, and the clock quietly went back to measuring downloads. Three things are corrected. Images no longer cancel each other, so a failure costs its own service and nothing else. A deployment now decides what to rebuild by comparing against **what is actually running in production** - recorded when a deployment succeeds - rather than against the previous set of changes, so anything that failed to ship is picked up by the next deployment instead of waiting for someone to edit it again. And the deployment's own health check now asks whether the address exists rather than merely whether the service answers the door: it accepted any reply at all, which is exactly how a service missing the endpoint passed as healthy. Found by reading a test client's console, where the rejections had been scrolling past all along
- **On the phone, every download button in the app did nothing at all - no file, no error, no trace.** Reported for the PDF on the first post of the feed, and it was never about PDFs: it was every download, everywhere. Saving a file on the web is done by handing the browser a link marked "download", and it is the **shell around the page** that acts on it - Chrome and Safari have a download manager, Android's WebView hands the request to a listener the host app has to install, and iOS needs a delegate. Tauri installs neither, so the click dispatched, succeeded, and produced nothing: not a failure that could be caught, logged or reported, just silence. Eleven places did it - a post's images, videos and files; a message's attachments and the media panel; the vault on the association page and the reviewer page; the .ics export; the form responses spreadsheet; the roster exports; the staged-attachment preview in the composer - and every one of them is now a single helper that writes through the OS's own save dialog on Tauri (Android's document picker, iOS's, the desktop save panel) and keeps the ordinary link on the web. It reports what it did, too: a confirmation on mobile, where there is no download shelf to notice, and an error toast instead of a swallowed exception. The permission this needs was the other half of the bug - Tauri gates every plugin command, `fs:default` is read-only, and a missing grant does not fail the build, it fails on a user's phone - so `fs:allow-write-file` is granted and pinned by the capability test, which now checks command names and not merely that the plugin is mentioned somewhere. The conversation backup export was the one path that had a mobile branch, and it asked for a *directory*, which Android's storage picker does not offer; it now goes through the same helper as everything else
- **Every download on Android and iOS failed with "Le telechargement a echoue", and the in-app PDF reader could not be zoomed with two fingers.** Two independent defects, both found on real hardware. The download failure was ours and not the platform's: on mobile the app replaces `window.fetch` with the Tauri HTTP plugin's, because the WebView's own client cannot reach a third-party origin from under the app's custom protocol - and the rule deciding which requests to keep native was written as a list of EXCEPTIONS. It named relative paths, the dev server and cookie-bearing calls, so anything it had not thought of went to the plugin, which is a network client living in a Rust thread and answers only `http` and `https`. Saving a decrypted attachment reads its object URL back, so every save handed a `blob:` URL to that client and got `scheme blob not supported` - a bare rejected promise, indistinguishable from the network being down, which is why the toast said the download had failed. The rule is now written the other way round: only an absolute `http(s)` URL means anything to the plugin, and every other scheme - `blob:`, `data:`, `filesystem:` - is the WebView's own to resolve. An exception list cannot be complete; a list of what the plugin actually implements can, and the decision is now a pure function with tests instead of a condition buried in a startup hook. Separately, the PDF reader had no pinch gesture at all - only its zoom buttons - and since the app disables the WebView's own page zoom, two fingers reached nothing. Pinching now tracks the gesture live as a transform and settles on release to the nearest zoom level, which is what re-rasterises the pages: scaling the bitmap during the gesture keeps it instant, and re-rendering at the settled width is what keeps the text sharp, that being the whole reason pages are rasterised rather than upscaled
- **"Reessayer" on the news feed fetched the posts and showed nothing; the only thing that worked was leaving the page and coming back.** Found from the user's own observation - the feed had failed to load once, the retry button did nothing however many times it was pressed, and switching tabs fixed it instantly. That last detail is the whole diagnosis: the initial list is a promise handed over by the route's load function while the page is already rendering, and a promise that has failed stays failed forever. The template waited on it and only drew the posts inside its success branch, so once it had failed the page was locked on the error screen - the retry did run, did reach the server and did get the posts back (measured on a phone: two hundred and a third of a second), and had nowhere to put them. Leaving and returning built a new promise, which is why it looked like the network. The refetched list is now read before that promise is consulted rather than inside it, so a successful retry is drawn whatever the first attempt did, and the retry shows the loading skeletons while it runs instead of leaving the error screen up with no sign anything is happening
- **Attaching a PDF showed an empty white rectangle instead of a preview, for everyone.** The strip of thumbnails above the message box - what you see after picking a file and before sending it - handed the PDF straight to the browser's built-in document plugin. The site's own security policy forbids that, deliberately and for good reason, so the browser blocked it every time, on every browser: what should have been the first page of the document was a blank tile, and the console carried a security warning on every attachment. It was the one place still doing it - every other PDF in the app, including the same file once it has been sent, is drawn by the app itself. It now uses that same drawing, so the preview appears, and falls back to the file icon and name when a document cannot be drawn at all
- **Pinching a PDF zoomed, but never where you were pointing.** The gesture shipped earlier the same day and did the hard part - it re-renders each page at the settled zoom rather than stretching a bitmap, so the text stays sharp - but it scaled the page column from its top edge and left the scroll where it was. The consequence on a phone, where the pinch is how you aim, is that the paragraph you put two fingers on slid away exactly as the zoom took hold, and you then had to hunt for it by dragging: the buttons were less annoying. The zoom now happens about the point between your fingers and stays there. Two halves make that true and both use the same point, so nothing jumps when one hands over to the other: during the gesture the live preview scales about the pinch centre instead of the top edge, and once the pages have been re-rendered at the new size the view is scrolled to put the same piece of the document back under the same finger. Zooming back out returns exactly where you started. The arithmetic - which is the only interesting part, the rest being plumbing - moved into a small tested module rather than staying inside the viewer, because the image viewer and the PDF viewer are supposed to end up sharing one gesture, and because a wrong scroll correction fails silently: a nonsensical value assigned to a scroll position is discarded by the browser without complaint. That correction then had to be done a second time, and the reason is worth recording because it is the kind of error a passing test cannot see. Computing the new scroll from the zoom ratio assumes the whole document scales together, and it does not: the gap between pages and the padding around them are fixed sizes that stay put while the pages grow. Measured on a phone at three times, the second page landed forty-eight pixels off, and one further gap's worth for every page deeper into the document - about two hundred by the eighth. So the viewer now remembers *which page* you pinched and where within it, measures that page again once it has been re-rendered, and scrolls by the difference it actually finds. - **The server's copy of a conversation now goes back several days instead of about one.** It holds a fixed number of entries per conversation, and that number had never been revisited: measured on 12 August, the busiest conversation's copy went back 22.6 hours - so a device switched off over a weekend came back to find the server could no longer help it, which is the exact case that copy exists for. Raised eightfold, together with the change above that puts reactions and edits into the same copy and would otherwise have shortened it further. The cost is bounded and small: the whole store was using 1.62 MB of the two gigabytes now allowed it
- **The server's copy of conversation history was never written to disk, and was destroyed every time its container was replaced.** Messages are stored on the server as ciphertext it cannot read, and that copy is what lets a device that has been away catch up on its own instead of having to ask another of your devices - which only works if one is online at that moment. It was being kept in memory inside the container, with no storage attached, so any deployment that replaced that container took the whole history of every conversation with it. Measured on 12 August: the container had last been replaced 31 hours earlier, and the server's copy of the busiest conversation went back exactly that far, while a phone in the same conversation still held twice as many messages. Nothing visible was lost, because each device keeps its own copy and they repair each other - but the repair needs a peer awake, and the server copy exists precisely so that it does not. It is now stored on disk, written as it arrives rather than snapshotted every few minutes. The rest of that database is untouched by the change and did not need to be kept: presence rebuilds itself when devices reconnect, and the routing table is rebuilt from the database whenever it is found empty
- **A failed disk write could freeze new messages from appearing for the rest of the session.** Two independent mechanisms are told about every catch-up phase - the one that saves the encryption state, and the one that batches the display. They were being run one after another with no isolation, so the first to fail stopped the second ever being told the phase had ended. In practice a full disk or a refused storage left the sync banner up indefinitely and, far worse, held incoming messages in a buffer instead of showing them, only to discard them later. The two are now genuinely independent: a failed save costs a save, never the reception.
- **The browser worked out that a message had been lost by reading its own log output.** A layer of the encryption core used to report "this message could not be read" only by printing a line to the console, so the app installed a hook over the log function and watched for the words `SecretReuseError` going past. That is not a contract, it is a leak - the app could not tell the difference between a message that was lost and a routine one with nothing to display, so it guessed from a string. The core reports the error properly now, which makes the whole hook unreachable: measured over a deliberate-break run, eleven detections arrived through the proper path and none through the log sniffer. Deleted, along with the patched global it installed. Nothing changes for anyone using the app; the same losses are detected the same way, by something that was designed to detect them.
- **A rule added at the network edge to loosen the site's security policy was silently tightening it instead, and had removed three capabilities the site itself grants.** Canari sends browsers a policy saying what the page is allowed to do, and it deliberately permits working with locally-held data - decrypted attachments, generated files, background workers - which the browser identifies by a scheme of its own rather than by an address. A second, apparently far more permissive policy was being added on top of it in front of the site. That cannot work, and cannot ever have worked: a browser applies every policy it is given separately and the page may only do what *all* of them allow, so a policy added on top can only ever take permissions away - never grant one. The added policy allowed "any address", which by definition covers addresses on the network and not locally-held data, so against a policy that names that case explicitly it removed it: reading generated files back, and starting a background worker from one, both stopped working on the web, while displaying an image from one kept working - which is precisely why nothing looked broken from the outside. Found in a browser console during testing rather than reported, and removed at the source; the site's own policy, which is versioned with the code and was correct throughout, is once again the only one in force
- **On Android, the app was hijacking a piece of the framework it runs on, and crippling itself for the rest of the session.** The mobile app routes its network calls through a native client rather than the WebView's own, because the WebView cannot reach the server from inside the app's private address space. The rule deciding which calls go that way said "anything that looks like a web address" - and the framework's own internal bridge, the channel the app and its native half talk to each other through, happens to look exactly like one. So every start, the very first message across that bridge was posted to the internet instead, to a name that exists nowhere; it failed, and the framework permanently fell back to its slower emergency channel for everything else that start. It was visible in the logs as a warning nobody had explained. The rule now recognises those internal addresses for what they are - a whole family of them, not the three that happen to exist today, since the framework can add more and the naming is reserved by standard for exactly this
- **The app cancelled a vibration nobody had started, every time it opened.** A safety step meant to silence the incoming-call buzz ran on every pass of the call-state check, including at startup with no call anywhere. Asking to stop a vibration is still asking to vibrate, and browsers refuse that before you have touched the screen - so two errors were logged on every single launch, for an operation that had nothing to cancel. It now does nothing at all unless something is actually ringing
- **A message helper reached back into the code that had loaded it, and which page you opened first decided whether that worked.** Adding the channel-reaction notification created a loop in the way the code loads itself: the piece that manages community channels asked for the messaging helpers, which - several steps later - asked for the piece that manages community channels. Loops like this do not fail on their own; they fail depending on which end is entered first, which is exactly the kind of behaviour that cannot be reproduced or explained. It was caught by the test suite rather than by anyone using the app. The notification call is now a small piece of its own, belonging to neither side, so there is no loop left to depend on

- **A message arriving during the few seconds you were being added to a conversation could be lost outright, with nothing anywhere to say so.** Being added to a conversation happens in two steps: your device receives an invitation that gives it what it needs to read that conversation, and only then can it open anything from it. Messages landing in between are set aside and applied straight after - otherwise they would be opened against a state the device does not have yet, which fails once and for good. Three different things emptied that holding area without ever applying what was in it, and none of them wrote a line anywhere. A second invitation for the same conversation - an ordinary event, since being re-added or simply being sent the invitation twice both produce one - replaced what was being held with nothing. An invitation that failed threw the held messages away, on the assumption the server would hand them over again, which is only true of a message the server still has a copy of - one delivered live, while you were connected, is held by nobody else. And an unrelated message of the same conversation failing released the holding area early, so what was in it was applied *before* the invitation that makes it readable, which fails in exactly the same way one step later. There is now a single way out, used by both outcomes, that always puts the messages back in the queue and always says what it did; the third path is simply gone, so the holding area belongs to the invitation that opened it from beginning to end. Alongside it, the app's own answer to "have I finished applying everything I received" now counts what is being held - it used to be able to say yes while messages sat aside, and it was only ever right because the three paths above were throwing them away. What makes that safe rather than a new way to freeze is a check at the end of every round: messages set aside with no invitation left anywhere to release them is a situation that cannot arise if the app is working, whatever the speed of anything - so it is reported as a fault, by name and by count, and the messages are put back rather than left there
### Security
- **Anybody in a conversation could delete or rewrite anybody else's messages, on every device in it.** Editing and deleting your own message works by sending the others a small instruction naming the message, and each device applied it by finding that message and changing it - by its identifier alone, without ever asking whether the person sending the instruction was the one who wrote it. The check existed, but on the wrong side of the wire: the app only offers the "edit" and "delete" buttons on your own messages, which decides what an honest device puts on the wire and decides nothing at all about what a dishonest one can. A single modified client could therefore have silently removed or reworded any message in any conversation or group it was a member of, on everyone's phone, with the change looking exactly like the author's own doing. Nothing else stood in the way: conversations and groups are end-to-end encrypted, so the server cannot read their contents and cannot police them the way it polices a channel. Each device now refuses an edit or a deletion that does not come from the message's author, and the identity it compares against is the one the encryption layer itself authenticated for that frame - a member can lie about which message it means, never about who it is. A refusal is silent on screen, since a peer sending something it had no right to send is not the reader's problem to act on, and never silent in the log. Found by audit, not in use
- **The same hole reopened one layer down, on the copy the server keeps.** Refusing an edit or a deletion that does not come from the message's author was implemented where those instructions arrive live. There is a second way they arrive: a device that has been away replays the conversation's stored copy, and applies everything in it. That path had no check at all - it never needed one, because until this release nothing that *changes* a message was ever stored, so there was never one to replay. Putting mutations into that copy - the change this release is built around, and the whole point of it - made those instructions replayable, and with them the ability to delete or reword any message in a conversation on any device that later caught up. Both paths now refuse a mutation from anyone but the author, and the second one had to learn a new trick to do it: an instruction can be replayed before the message it applies to, so it now records who claimed it and the check is made against the real author once the message itself is in place. Found while verifying the replay path rather than by a test, which is the reason it is worth saying that this code had never had one
- **The server let a member write into a conversation's stored copy under another member's name.** Everything a device sends carries who sent it, and the server writes that name into the copy it keeps - which is what a device replaying that copy attributes each message, and each edit or deletion in it, to. That name was taken from the message itself and never compared against the account the request was authenticated as, so a modified client could have added anything to a conversation's history in someone else's name: words they never wrote, or - now that instructions travel in that copy too - a deletion of their own messages that every device would honour. The two are now required to match, on the same route every other request in that service already checked. The background path used when the app is closed was already sound: it proves who it is with a per-device secret before its name is used. Found by audit, not in use
- **The site accepted connections over two obsolete versions of TLS.** The minimum version a browser could negotiate with canari-emse.fr was TLS 1.0 - a protocol deprecated for years and with known weaknesses, kept alive only for browsers that no longer exist. Every browser and both mobile runtimes Canari supports have spoken TLS 1.2 for over a decade, so nothing reachable was relying on it. The floor is now 1.2 and the two older versions are refused outright

### Removed
- **The QR code that transferred your conversations to another device is gone, and nothing replaces it because nothing needs to.** It asked you to open one device, show a code, scan it with the other and wait - and it did not work: the server required a `salt` field in every manifest upload that the app never sent, so any transfer carrying actual messages was rejected outright, and the two public keys exchanged to set the session up were never read by anything, no key agreement existing anywhere along that path. What made it worth deleting rather than repairing is that the account's devices now pool their history by themselves, comparing what each holds and exchanging only the difference, with no gesture from anyone. The section in the settings keeps the half that was always sound - exporting your conversations to an encrypted `.canari` file and restoring one - and is renamed to say so, since a panel headed "Backup & sync" whose description offered to "transfer your conversations to another device (QR)" would have been advertising something that no longer exists. Eight server endpoints, the session engine, the scanner modal and twenty-two now-orphaned interface strings go with it
- **A conversation that lost a message used to answer by shouting, and it could not stop.** When a device failed to decrypt something - because the sender's own encryption state had gone backwards, after a reload or between two tabs - it asked every member to re-send everything from the last two minutes. It could not do better: the message had never been decrypted, so its identity was never known, and a request that cannot name what it wants can only ask for a period of time. Measured on production, three devices in one conversation held about 450 frames a minute for over ten minutes, with nobody typing and nothing being repaired - and the only way that repair ever succeeded was by accident, the sender eventually encrypting past the point of collision while it answered. It is deleted rather than tuned, and nothing is lost with it: the devices already compare what they hold and exchange precisely the difference, naming each message, which repairs the same loss exactly and costs nothing when there is none. Removing it also removes an in-memory copy of every message sent in the last five minutes

### Fixed
- **Tapping a message notification while the app was closed opened Canari, but not the conversation - it landed on the feed and stayed there.** With the app merely in the background the same tap worked perfectly, which is what kept this hidden: whichever way you check it after using the app, it behaves. Tauri requires each capability a plugin exposes to be granted explicitly, and the deep-link plugin had **no grant at all**: it was declared as a dependency, configured with its five link types, and used by the startup code, but the permission list never mentioned it. Events are not gated, so the running case worked and the cold start asked its question and was refused - every time, on both platforms. The refusal was then swallowed by an empty error handler, making "I was refused" and "nothing opened me" the same silent outcome, so no log on any device said anything was wrong. The permission is now granted - exactly the one command a cold start needs, and nothing else - and the failure is logged rather than discarded. A new test asserts that every plugin the app depends on is granted somewhere, since nothing else checks it: an ungranted plugin builds, ships and installs perfectly, and only fails on a real device, at the moment a user taps something. If Canari was started by tapping a notification, the system keeps handing the app that notification's target for as long as the app is running - it means "the last thing you were sent here for", not "you were just sent here". The app remembered it had already acted on it, but it remembered in a place that a reload wipes, so any reload asked the question again and got the same fifteen-minute-old answer: it navigated you away from whatever you were doing and into the old conversation. The reloads that reach this in normal use are the "recharger" button on the encryption error banner - so someone repairing a problem was rewarded by being teleported - the update prompt, and the manual sync reset. The app now remembers what it has handled for as long as the session it belongs to, which means a genuine new tap still opens its conversation while a reload no longer replays a spent one.
- **A message you sent while your app was catching up disappeared from your own screen at the next load - the person you wrote to still had it.** Nobody echoes your own message back to you: the copy that appears in your conversation the instant you press send is written once, locally, and that write is the only one it will ever get. It was being routed through the buffer that exists to stop a large catch-up re-rendering the list message by message - a buffer that holds messages aside and returns *before* anything is saved, and that is emptied without being flushed whenever a second catch-up starts or the catch-up state is reset. The outbox could not repair it either: it looks the message up among the loaded conversations to mark it sent, and a buffered message never got there, so it gave up without writing. Because that buffer is raised on **every** inbound drain - not only on a long one - any message composed during a sync window died before reaching storage: present on the recipient, gone from the sender at the next reload, with nothing logged. It also explains why the same thing sent with no connection was always correct - offline there is no inbound drain, so the local copy took the ordinary path. Your own message is now never held aside; one extra item costs nothing to render, and the buffer's purpose was never a single local send. Both places that empty the buffer also say what they are discarding, since a dropped buffer and a message that never arrived are otherwise indistinguishable. Found by the cross-client test campaign's reconciliation pass, which compares the two sides of the same conversation - the only thing that can see a loss of this shape
- **A device you deleted could come back by itself, and then be sent every message forever with no way for anyone to stop it.** Deleting a device erases everything the server holds for it and blacklists it, so it can never register again. But one endpoint could re-create its routing record from nothing: the one a device (or a peer whose copy of the conversation still lists it) calls to say "this device is already in the conversation". It checked neither the blacklist nor whether the device still existed, while the endpoint next to it - the one that hands out invitations - checks both. So a deleted device that was still installed re-listed itself in every conversation it remembered, and from then on every message sent to that conversation was stored for it as well, forever: it was invisible in the device list (which is built from data the deletion had erased), invisible to the cleanup jobs (built from the same data), and it could not be deleted a second time because it could not be seen. Measured on production: **nine such devices held 97 353 of the 98 210 undelivered messages** - 99.1 %, about 150 MB - and one account had five of them, so every message it received was stored six times over. One of the nine had been deleted on 31 July and was still having records written for it on 4 August. Removing them through the app took the table to 984 rows. Four things changed: a device that may not be invited may not be vouched for either, and the same check now also guards the two other paths that could promote a device (a device sending a conversation-key change, and the background push path); the cleanup job that reclaims dead devices now finds those with no server-side identity at all, which it structurally could not before; the job that decides a device is gone no longer trusts a timestamp that *other people's* clients keep refreshing, and uses the device's own last connection instead; and messages are no longer stored for a device that has no server-side identity, with a log line naming it when that happens. The phone handled that signal *after* trying to decrypt it - and returned early when the decryption failed, which is precisely what happens when the phone has been closed long enough to fall behind. The dismissal never needed the decrypted content: which conversation, and whether the signal came from you, are both sent in the clear. - **A message that could not be decrypted because the device was too far behind was deleted from the server as if it had been delivered.** When a device misses a long enough run of messages from one sender - which is exactly what the undrainable backlog above produced - the encryption keys run too far ahead for it to catch up, and the next message fails to decrypt with an error meaning "this is further ahead than I can follow". That is a different failure from the one it was read as. The app has a repair for being *behind on group changes*: fetch the group changes it missed and re-apply them. So it ran that repair, found there were no group changes to apply (there was no such gap - the device was at the group's current state all along), and computed its verdict as "am I at the current state?" - trivially yes. The deadline now belongs to **one page** rather than to the whole read, and each page is handed to the decryption queue as it lands - so it is acknowledged, and removed from the queue server-side, before the next page is requested. An attempt that dies half-way now leaves the backlog smaller than it found it, and the next one resumes from there. The log says how far it got, because "the fetch failed" and "the fetch failed after draining four pages" are the difference between a device that is stuck and one that is catching up. The two are now told apart on the evidence available: the frame's own bytes. The consequence is that a page going away before the next checkpoint restores, on the following load, a ratchet **behind** the one already used - so the next message is encrypted at a generation the peer has already seen. An unload hook is a best-effort extra, never the guarantee, so the checkpoint now happens where the ratchet actually moved, right after the send. Same-tick sends still merge into one write and a bulk ingest still defers, so a burst costs one checkpoint rather than one per message. Verified against production with the reproduction that found it: three rounds of send, 300 ms, reload, send now deliver 3 out of 3 in about 700 ms - the same latency as a round with no reload at all, where the previous build lost 2 out of 2

The entry sat a release behind until someone happened to run cargo, and then the pre-commit sweep carried the regenerated lock into whatever commit was in flight - `0.11.7 -> 0.12.0` rode in on an update feature, `0.12.0 -> 0.13.0` on a **documentation** commit (`0e86b34c`). ## [v0.13.0] - 2026-08-06

### Added
- **Canari can now actually be found, not just previewed.** The head written for unfurlers was only half the audience: a search engine reads the same bytes, and for it the situation was worse than a poor preview. Googlebot does execute JavaScript, but it crawls as an anonymous visitor - so on a SPA whose content sits behind a login, what it renders is the sign-in screen. Not a thin page: an empty one. Which makes the injected `<head>` not an enhancement on top of indexable content but the **entire indexable surface of the site**, and that reframes what belongs in it. So the head now carries **JSON-LD** describing what the page is: `Article` for a post, with its real headline, its publication date and its author (an `Organization` when an association wrote it, a `Person` otherwise - an association is not a person, and saying so lets the node reconcile with the one its own page declares), `Organization` for an association, `ItemList` of `Event` for the agenda, and a `BreadcrumbList` on every nested page, which is what turns a result's URL line from a bare uuid path into `canari-emse.fr > Associations > BDE`. Events are the one type here with a real chance of a rich result - Google renders them with their dates - and an agenda is exactly what a student searches for, so every event declares the `location` Google's guidelines warn about. Threaded through all of it is the school: "Canari" on its own is a bird and cannot be won as a query, and the only thing that disambiguates it is being attached, consistently and in a form Google already knows, to Mines Saint-Etienne by name, URL and postal address - so one `institutionNode()` hangs off every graph and the association pages title themselves `<name> - Mines Saint-Etienne`. Serialising that is security-critical and gets its own escaper: `JSON.stringify` leaves `</script>` byte-for-byte intact, and inside a script element that sequence ends the element, so a post title alone would be an injection point - `serializeJsonLd` escapes `<` and `&` as unicode escapes, and a test proves an attacking title survives the round trip as data. Second, **the sitemap is now built per request**, because on this site it is not a convenience but the entire link graph: nothing links to anything in served HTML, so a crawler cannot walk from `/associations` to `/associations/bde` and the eight static paths listed before were all it could ever discover. It now merges in every non-archived association and the recent posts - from `feed=associations`, deliberately, not `feed=all`: both are readable without a session, but submitting a URL to a search engine is a different act from not blocking it, and an association's post is a communication its authors want found where a student's personal post is not something to put in front of Google on their behalf. Both halves run in parallel and may come back empty, since a short sitemap is worth serving and a 500 is not. Third, and the reason the first two are not quietly undone: hydration used to **replace** the server's head with what the browser could work out alone, which for `/associations/bde` is the slug and a generic sentence. An unfurler never gets that far, but a crawler does, and it would index the downgrade. The injected block now ends with the resolved metadata as JSON, which `SeoHead` reads back once (memoised, because it deletes the element on mount) and adopts with the highest precedence - keyed on the requested path, not the canonical one, since `/` canonicalises to `/posts` and keying on that would stop the client ever recognising its own payload. Smaller things in the same pass: `article:published_time` and `article:author`, `og:image:alt` only where the image's contents are actually known (the default alt text describes the Canari bird - printing it over an association's logo is a wrong answer where none is a correct one), a `WebSite.potentialAction` declaring the search entry point a sitelinks search box is built from, and `/calendar` and `/shop` named in `robots.txt`. Documented in the new `docs/wiki/frontend/seo.md`
- **The SSR container being down no longer takes the whole site with it.** Shipping the server-rendered head swapped `adapter-static` for `adapter-node` on the web build, and `adapter-node` emits no `index.html` - so nginx had nothing to answer with and a dead `frontend-ssr` meant a **502 on every navigation**. That traded the entire site for a `<head>`, on an app that is a SPA and needs no server to run at all; before the SEO work the same outage was not even possible. `routes/app-shell/` is now prerendered into `build/prerendered/app-shell.html` - a plain shell that boots the SPA on whatever URL was requested, `kit.start(app, element)` with no route data, exactly the shape the static build's fallback always had - and nginx serves it from a named `@app_shell` location on 502/503/504. Deliberately **without** the `=` on the `error_page`, so the 5xx status is preserved while the body is the shell: a browser runs the scripts of a 5xx body, so a person gets a working app, while a crawler gets a status it will retry rather than a 200 carrying `noindex`, which is a request to *deindex* the page. The degraded answer carries `X-Canari-Degraded: ssr-unavailable` and `no-store`, so the outage is greppable in the access log, and it stays visible in `docker compose ps` through the container's healthcheck. What is lost while degraded is exactly the per-page head - the bonus the container exists to add
- **A link preview no longer tells the site who is reading, and stops asking it the same question on every render.** Four changes to the same card, one of them a privacy fix worth stating first: the `og:image` and the favicon were loaded by an `<img src>` pointing straight at the remote host, so every reader of a message opened a connection to a third party **from inside an end-to-end encrypted conversation** - the site learned each reader's IP, their user agent, and the moment they scrolled the message into view. Encrypting the body and then fetching its illustration in clear hands a good part of that back. `GET /api/mls/link-preview/image?url=` now fetches it server-side through the same SSRF guard as the page itself, relays `image/*` only (SVG excluded - it is a document that can carry script and would be served from our own origin, with `nosniff` on the way out), caps the body at 3 MB on both the declared and the received length, and follows redirects rather than walking them by hand since a CDN answers with two or three and the dispatcher re-validates every connect anyway. The client rewrite is one helper applied to the image and to **every** favicon candidate, the conventional paths being derived in the browser where nothing server-side could have rewritten them; URLs already on our own origin are skipped, which is what keeps the MiGallery cover proxy from being proxied twice. The CSP `img-src` is deliberately left alone - Klipy GIFs and other remote sources need auditing first, and that is its own change. Second, the endpoint had **no cache of any kind**: a conversation scrolled back through re-downloaded every previewed page, every time, on every device. Two bounded LRU caches now sit in front of it - 6 h on a page that answered, 10 min on one that did not (a site that was merely down is retried the same day rather than written off), 6 h for a proxied image and only when it is under 256 KB, since a favicon is asked for constantly and costs a kilobyte while a full-size `og:image` would evict a hundred of them to save one request - with a matching `Cache-Control`, which is the only thing that stops the browser asking at all. Per-process on purpose: the service runs as a single instance, and replicating it would cost hit rate, never correctness. Third, a page that declares an oEmbed endpoint is now followed, which covers Spotify, Vimeo, Bandcamp, X and the rest through one code path instead of a short-circuit per site; discovery is free (the declaration is in HTML we already hold) and the merge is written so it can be applied blindly - Open Graph wins wherever both speak, and oEmbed only fills gaps: a title where the page fell back to its hostname, a thumbnail where there was no `og:image`, and `author_name`, the one thing Open Graph has no field for, which becomes the description when the page declared none. The YouTube short-circuit stays ahead of it, needing no HTML fetch at all. Fourth, the four sites we share users with - MiGallery, Sky, Le Cercle, Portail Etu - are named rather than spelled out: their chip says "Le Cercle" instead of `cercle.canari-emse.fr`, which names the destination to nobody but whoever deployed it. That lives in one registry which also decides which paths deserve the cover-first card, replacing a hostname compared in place in two files, so a fifth site is one entry rather than a second copy of a component. It carries no bundled logos on purpose - the card already resolves each site's own favicon, which survives a rebrand
- **A Canari link pasted into Discord, Slack or iMessage now unfurls with the page's own title, description and image.** The whole `src/lib/seo/` tree - canonical URLs, JSON-LD, per-page descriptions, `noindex` rules, the sitemap - was written for crawlers and unfurlers, and not one of them ever saw a byte of it: `SeoHead.svelte` renders inside a `<svelte:head>` on a client where `export const ssr = false`, so the server answered every path with the same `app.html` shell and its one generic description, and the real tags only appeared after hydration, which no unfurler performs. The fix does **not** turn SSR on - no component renders server-side, so nothing about the SPA, the MLS worker, the stores or Tauri changes. The web build simply gains a Node process: `svelte.config.js` now picks `adapter-node` when `BUILD_WEB=1` (set only by the `build-frontend` job in `cd.yml`) and keeps `adapter-static` otherwise, the polarity chosen so a build that forgets the variable produces the static bundle Tauri needs rather than a server it cannot consume. `hooks.server.ts` then rewrites two literal markers in the shell - `<title>Canari</title>` and a new `<!--canari-seo-->` - via `transformPageChunk`, which fires under `ssr = false`; a test asserts both markers still exist in `app.html`, since the injector would otherwise no-op in silence. Six path kinds are enriched with their real data (`/posts/:id`, `/forms/:id`, `/associations/:slug`, `/profile/:id`, and both invite links, `/c/join/:token` and `/g/join/:token` - the last two by design, an invitation being useless if it does not say what it invites you to), each read straight from social-service, core-service or chat-delivery over the Docker network with `X-Internal-Secret` behind a 1.5 s timeout, never through nginx and never with `X-Internal-Token`, whose HMAC is bound to a user id and would be impersonation rather than authentication. Anything that fails - a service down, an unknown path, a deleted post - falls back to the baseline `resolveSeoForPath` answer, because a page must never fail because its preview did, and a 60 s LRU absorbs the burst of unfurlers one share produces. Every interpolated value goes through a single escaper with its own test file: post text and association names land inside HTML attributes in the app shell, which is precisely where an unescaped quote becomes an injection. `og:image` is built absolute from the site origin rather than through `associationLogoSrc()`, which falls back to `http://localhost:3011` when `window` is undefined - exactly the case here - and its `width`/`height` are emitted only for the default image, an entity image's dimensions being unknown. Hydration cleans up after the server: the injected tags carry `data-canari-seo` and `SeoHead` removes them on mount, so a browsing user never holds two `og:title`s. Infrastructure-wise the web front is now **two containers over one build artifact** - nginx keeps every `/api/*` location, the CSP and the static assets with their proven cache headers and the `.mjs` `default_type` fix, and forwards only HTML navigations to `frontend-ssr` through a named `@ssr` upstream. Stated plainly because it is a real change in failure mode: `adapter-node` emits no `index.html`, so there is no static fallback and a navigation answers 502 if `frontend-ssr` is down. Both images are built from the same artifact and the CD deploys them together for that reason
- **Canari opens with no network, and settles the session when the network returns.** A cold start in a metro, a plane or a dead zone could not get past the unlock screen: it showed "impossible de joindre le serveur" while a complete encrypted history sat on the device, perfectly readable. The blocker was never the PIN - it was `getToken()`. The access token lives in memory only, so every cold start goes to `POST /api/auth/refresh`, and offline that `fetch` rejects before a single local byte is read. **Only the two paths that already skip the server PIN check when online may now unlock without it** - biometrics, whose factor is the platform keystore, and the "rester connecté" device-key vault - so an offline unlock verifies exactly what an online one verifies, and no new secret is written anywhere. The PIN path is deliberately left refusing: its at-rest key derives from a per-user salt only the server holds, and caching that salt is precisely what would turn a 4-character PIN into an offline-bruteforceable secret against `mls.bin`. The distinction that makes the whole thing safe is between a server *answer* and a *transport failure*: a refresh answering 401/403 means we reached the server and it refused us, which still signs the user out, while a rejected socket means it said nothing at all. `_doRefresh` separates them at the source, and a new connectivity store keeps the two facts apart for everyone else, because `navigator.onLine` is optimistic - a captive portal reports `true` - so being offline also requires that the last call actually failed to arrive. An offline session runs on an empty token and skips the gateway connect, the push registration, group discovery and both watchdogs; the connection watchdog especially, which left running would schedule a reconnect every tick, burn its twenty attempts against a network that is not there and leave the circuit open, so regaining signal would land the user on a "Réessayer" button instead of a working app. When connectivity comes back, one single-flight promotion does the whole sequence in order: acquire the token (a dead session logs out here, keeping the local encrypted store, so signing back in restores the history), re-register the push token since FCM may have rotated it, reopen the WebSocket and drain everything the server queued, **then** flush the outbox - its own `online` listener would otherwise have fired first with an empty token and burnt one failed attempt and one backoff step on every queued message - and finally re-solicit the histories that gave up while offline. Reading works from the local store, writing queues in the existing durable outbox, and a banner says which of the two states you are in. Device revocation is not deferred by any of this: `resetRequired` only ever travels through `pin-check`, which a biometric or vault login does not call even when online, so that gap predates offline unlock and is still closed on reconnect by the refresh and the gateway handshake
- **WP-SESS-2: a Canari session can finally be revoked, and "Connexions actives" in the settings is where you do it.** Nothing could end a login. The refresh token was a self-describing 7-day bearer credential verified by signature alone - no `jti`, no store, no reuse detection - so `logout` cleared the cookie and revoked nothing (the token it had just "revoked" kept working from anywhere else), and a stolen cookie was worth seven days *and* minted a fresh seven on every use, in parallel with the real user and unnoticed. The only lever that existed was rotating `JWT_SECRET`, which signs every user out of all six services at once. Each login now owns a row in `auth_sessions`, and the refresh cookie carries `sid` (the row) and `jti` (the only token that row accepts), so a valid signature is no longer sufficient: the token must also name a live session and be the one it expects. `logout` deletes the row, the settings panel lists every session with its device, its last activity and its IP and kills any of them - or all the others at once - and a replayed `jti` **destroys** the session rather than logging it, because detecting a replay and only logging it rotates the token for whoever presented it, which is the theft succeeding with an alarm attached. That rule needed a companion to be safe: two tabs share one cookie, so a simultaneous refresh is routine, exactly one wins the rotation and the loser presents a token one generation old through nobody's fault - `previousTokenId` + `rotatedAt` keep the replaced token acceptable for 60 seconds, during which the server hands back the current token without rotating again. The rotation is one conditional `UPDATE ... WHERE "tokenId" = :presented`, so the race is settled by Postgres rather than by a read-then-write. Deliberately **not** built: a `JWT_OLD_SECRET` grace window, whose second step is invisible and therefore never taken - Le Cercle's production is the standing proof, its old secret still signing months later - and which is backwards for the case you actually rotate in, a leak; and a stateful access token, which would put a database round trip in front of all six services and the nginx `auth_request`, so the 1-hour access token stays stateless and the panel says out loud that access already granted survives up to an hour. Tokens issued before this release carry no `sid` and are adopted into a fresh session instead of being refused, which would have signed out every logged-in user on the deploy; they grant nothing new and cannot exist past one refresh TTL. Two smaller holes closed on the way, both from one key signing both token kinds: a refresh token presented as a Bearer token passed `/api/auth/verify` and authenticated its holder, and would have passed the new session routes too. No CI/CD change was needed - `cd.yml` already fails on a missing `JWT_SECRET`, upserts it, and re-reads it from inside the running container to compare its sha256 against the GitHub secret, so a rotation that did not land fails the deploy; what was missing was a written cadence, now in `docs/wiki/cicd.md`
- **A test button on `/admin/cercle` runs a Cercle top-up end to end without charging a card.** Preparing the first real recharge meant either wiring a live Stripe payment or trusting that the dispatcher works, and neither proves the deployment. Each `balance_topup` product now carries a button that credits 5 EUR to the pressing admin's own Cercle account through the production path: `resolvePurchase` for the buyer gating, the purchase limits and the server-side amount, then `handlePurchaseCompleted` - the very entry point core-service calls from the Stripe webhook - so the signed dispatch, its three-attempt ladder, the `webhook_deliveries` audit row and the `purchase_records` row are the real ones rather than a parallel implementation that would only ever prove itself. The panel reports what the dispatcher recorded, and a failure lands in the retry list below it like any other. Three departures, each deliberate: the PaymentIntent is synthetic and prefixed `pi_canari_test_`, which is also the idempotency key the Cercle stores, so the rows it leaves are identifiable on both sides; the two conditions that exist purely to take money - the product being on sale, the Connect account being onboarded - are waived, because an association with no Stripe account must still be able to prove its webhook reaches the Cercle, and Le Cercle has no Stripe account today; and a product with an empty `webhookUrl`/`webhookSecret` is refused up front rather than run, since the real fulfillment skips the dispatch silently there and the test would otherwise report a flawless success while nothing was ever sent. It does record a genuine 5 EUR purchase in the association's accounting, which is what reproducing everything means. Global admin only, and the beneficiary is always the caller - the endpoint reads `x-user-id`, never a body field, so it cannot become a way to hand out balance
- **A cotisant's forfait can be changed from the roster.** A multi-tier association could only ever assign a tier at the moment it added someone: correcting a mistake, or moving a cotisant from sans-alcool to avec-alcool, meant revoking them and adding them back. The tier badge is now a picker, and switching it re-grants through `grantCotisant` rather than through a new endpoint - granting already revokes the sibling tiers in the same transaction, so upgrade and downgrade are the same operation and there is no window in which the cotisant holds two forfaits or none
- **`/admin/cercle` now describes one top-up rather than a product catalogue.** The page offered a full boutique CRUD - create as many `balance_topup` products as you like, each with a fixed price, a stock limit and its own webhook - for a thing that exists exactly once: the recharge of an account on the Cercle's site. It is now a single configuration form for the selected beneficiary's one product (amount bounds, webhook URL, webhook secret), with the shape a recharge actually has baked in rather than offered as choices: the buyer picks the amount, it can be bought any number of times, and it can never be out of stock (the server forces `allowRepeatPurchase` and clears both purchase caps). The test button follows: one general button under a "Tester le lien avec Le Cercle" heading instead of one per row, disabled with an explanation until a URL and a secret are set. Le Cercle is preselected on arrival, the selector staying for whoever else ends up with a balance one day

A name wraps on spaces, so what has to fit a line is its longest single word, and the ladder had no idea one was there: "Elliot WAGHEMACKER" is 18 characters and got the size an 18-character name gets, while the 11-character surname it contains needed 66 poster px in a 61 px box, so the browser split the word rather than the name. `memberCardMetrics` now answers the three problems in order, because they are genuinely different: a long full name wraps over more lines, so it starts smaller (the ladder, now a touch tighter overall as asked); a single long surname cannot wrap at all, so the font shrinks until that word fits one line; and past a readable floor, shrinking further is worse than a wider plate, so the CARD widens instead - up to 1.4x, centered on its slot, growing symmetrically so nothing else on the unit moves. Only the reads were loud. What made it read as cosmetic is that the conversation *list* came back fine - conversation metadata rows are plaintext - so the app looked right and was quietly discarding everything that arrived. `initialiser_mls` already caches the resolved key natively, so that saves arriving later with an empty key still work; a new `recuperer_cle_session_mls` returns that cached copy and `loginImpl` puts it in the session before anything reads it. It deliberately reads the cache rather than the keystore: a second read would raise a second biometric prompt in the middle of a login that already succeeded, and a test pins it to the cache for that reason. The key stops there - the local variable that gates the device-key vault write and `store_push_context` stays empty, because the keystore is where this key belongs at rest - and a keystore that answers with nothing now fails the login onto the PIN modal instead of continuing, since continuing means another session that persists nothing with nothing in the log. `TauriMlsService` takes the same answer for its own copy, because the resume-time reload of `mls.bin` skips when it has no key, and skipping it is how a background engine's advance gets clobbered by the next save
- **The login log named the wrong storage backend.** "MLS state loaded from IndexedDB." on a mobile launch that had just read `mls.bin` - `loadMlsState` branches on the runtime and calls `load_mls_state` under Tauri, but the caller labelled its result `indexeddb` and kept a second, Tauri-only branch for the native case that could therefore never be the one to answer. The source is now taken from the runtime, the dead retry is gone, and the message says `mls.bin (native)`. Nothing changes about where the state is stored; this is only a log line that lied to whoever was reading it to work out whether local storage was healthy - which is precisely when it was read
- **Biometric unlock never worked on the first attempt, only on the second.** After a restart the sheet appeared, no system prompt followed it, and the app fell through to the PIN modal - where tapping "use biometrics" worked immediately, which made it look like a race. It was deterministic. `loginImpl` refuses to run when `isLoginInProgress` is set, and `startLoginFlow` sets that very flag before any `await` for an unrelated reason: `+layout.ts` must not fire `fetchUserProfile` while a login is pending. One variable, two purposes, so every entry point that calls into `loginImpl` has to release it first - the web branch did, with a comment saying why, and `nativeStorageLogin` did; the biometric branch did not. The automatic attempt of every cold launch was therefore refused by the guard it had itself tripped, logging `[LOGIN] Call ignored, a login already owns the flow`, and the fall-through to the PIN modal cleared the flag on its way past, which is what made the manual retry succeed. That fall-through is also why the bug read as harmless for so long: it always ended somewhere usable. The biometric entry point now hands the flag over exactly like the web branch, and re-entrancy stays guarded by the component-local flag that was always doing that job

### Security
- **The link-preview SSRF guard could be walked past three ways.** `/api/mls/link-preview` fetches a user-supplied URL from inside the cluster, and `isPrivateIpAddress` is the whole decision - it is consulted both before the fetch and again at connect time by the undici dispatcher, so an address it calls public is one the socket reaches. It called three families of address public. `0.0.0.0` was not in the IPv4 list at all, and under Linux a connection to `0.0.0.0` lands on loopback, so `http://0.0.0.0:8080/` reached any service bound to the wildcard address; `::` was the same hole on IPv6. Worse, the IPv6 branch only recognised `::1`, `fc`/`fd` and the literal `fe80:` prefix, so every IPv4-mapped form was waved through: `::ffff:127.0.0.1` and its hex spelling `::ffff:7f00:1` are addresses whose socket lands on loopback, and Node's resolver hands back exactly that shape - both barriers judged them public. And because `URL.hostname` keeps the brackets around an IPv6 literal, `isIP('[::1]')` answered "not an address" and the literal check was skipped entirely, leaving only the DNS path to catch it by accident. The predicate is now split per family and widened to the blocks a server-side fetch has no business reaching - `0.0.0.0/8`, CGNAT, the protocol and benchmarking assignments, multicast and reserved space on IPv4; the unspecified address, the full `fe80::/10` rather than one hextet of it, and multicast on IPv6 - while any address embedding an IPv4 (`::ffff:`, `::`, NAT64, in either notation) is judged on the embedded address, which is where the socket actually goes. Hosts are unbracketed before the literal check, an out-of-range octet now counts as malformed, and malformed still means private: an address that cannot be classified is not one to connect to

The foreground plugin was outside the guardrail, so the fix landed on one twin and the newline lived on in the other. `DEFAULT` remains correct where those two files use it for the IV and ciphertext, which never leave Kotlin; the rule is about the wire, not about base64. The inviter's copy is now inserted locally at send time, and it is the same amber card as the invitee's rather than a grey one-liner: "Vous avez invité X à rejoindre cette communauté", with no Join button since they are already a member. `channelInvite.invitedName` is the discriminator, and its presence is what suppresses the button. While in there, the workspace name stopped defaulting to the hardcoded English literal `'the community'`, which leaked untranslated into the invitee's card whenever the workspace name was unknown - it is left undefined and every consumer already falls back to the channel name

- **The one-way colour sweep is finished: 390 occurrences down to 31, and the 31 are deliberate.** The status triad is complete - `--amber-warn` joins `--green-ok` and `--red-err`, so the amber/emerald/green/sky/orange warning banners stop hardcoding `bg-amber-50` / `border-amber-200` / `text-amber-700`, none of which flip. `-600` shades went with them: `text-red-600` reads fine on a light card and drops to about 3.9:1 once the card flips, where `text-red-err` stays above 7:1. Every remaining raw dark hex is now a token too - `[#0f1622]` was literally `--cn-surface`, and the surfaces that are meant to stay dark in both themes (call chrome, tooltips) became the fixed `cn-scrim` / `cn-tooltip`, the same idea as `cn-ink`. Found along the way: `bg-cn-surface-alt` was used by six components but never defined in `@theme`, so Tailwind generated nothing and those inactive badges rendered with no background at all. The detector is now committed at `frontend/scripts/find-oneway-colors.mjs` instead of living in a commit message, and it stays quiet about the two idioms that are theme-neutral on purpose: black scrims, and white at 20% opacity or less (glassmorphism). What it still reports is intentional - switch thumbs, colour-picker handles, the always-dark lightbox, and the white plate behind a QR code, which has to be white to scan

- **The two biggest families of one-way colour are gone.** A one-way utility is one with no `dark:` counterpart in the same class list: it does not flip, while the `text-text-main` on top of it does, so the pair degrades to white-on-white exactly where the theme changes. Every raw `[#151B2C]` (47 sites) is now the `cn-ink` token - identical colour, since `--color-cn-ink` *is* `#151b2c`, but it can no longer drift from it - and the red error banner triad (`bg-red-50` / `border-red-200` / `text-red-700`, ~120 sites) now draws from `red-err`, which flips on its own; the `dark:bg-red-900` overrides that used to compensate were dropped with it. Amber, emerald and the remaining dark hexes are still to do. Worth recording: a plain grep over-reports this by 4x, because `bg-white dark:bg-slate-900` is perfectly fine - the detection has to be per class list, and must not tokenize on `:` or it strips the very `dark:` prefixes it is looking for
- **Revoking a device now asks before it fires.** The trash icon in "Gestion des appareils" ran `deleteDevice` on a single click, and that call is a full purge - KeyPackages, prekeys, push tokens, group memberships, queued messages, Redis routing entry - so anything still in flight for that device was lost with no way back and no warning. Kicking a member from a channel already goes through `showConfirm`; revoking a device is at least as destructive and now gets the same treatment, naming the device and stating what is lost. The dialog renders above the device modal (`z-[300]` over `z-[280]`)
- **"Rester connecte" described a secret that is no longer stored.** Both the settings row and the PIN sheet still promised to keep the PIN ("Conserve votre PIN", "Votre PIN sera conserve"), copy written before v0.11.0 moved the at-rest secret to a 32-byte device key. The PIN has not been persisted since; what the toggle actually does is move the AES-GCM-wrapped device key from `sessionStorage` to `localStorage`. Misleading copy on a security control is a defect in the control, so both strings now say what is kept and add that the PIN itself never is
- The device list no longer hardcodes "Appareil {n}" in French - it goes through Paraglide like every other user-visible string

### Security
- **A community slug was enough to read every one of its channel keys.** `GET /api/channels/workspaces/by-slug/:slug` returned the raw TypeORM `Channel` entities, and a channel entity carries `masterSecret` - the 32-byte HKDF root that every epoch key of that channel is derived from. It also ran with no membership check whatsoever, so any authenticated Canari user who knew a slug got, for a community they had never joined: its full member roster, its roles, its private channels, and the key material to decrypt the entire history of all of them. A slug is not a secret - every invite link contains one and the invite preview hands it back before you join. The endpoint now requires membership, returns only the channels the caller may actually read (`canAccessChannel`, same rule as the sidebar listing, archived ones excluded), and projects those channels field by field instead of serializing the entity. **Rotating the affected channels' `masterSecret` is not automatic** - a rotation re-keys the channel and is a deliberate act; the exposure window is any deployment before this one
- **An association admin could revoke any other association's cotisants.** `DELETE /api/associations/:id/tags/:tagId` took both parameters and used only the second: `UserTagService.revoke` deleted by primary key with no ownership check, while the guard in front of it verified MANAGE_MEMBERS on `:id` - a permission granted per association. So MANAGE_MEMBERS anywhere, plus a tag id, revoked a cotisation issued anywhere else, and the roster hands those ids out to whoever can read it. The delete is now scoped to `issuingAssocId`, which makes `:id` the authorization rather than decoration: a tag belonging to another association answers 404, exactly as if it did not exist
- **iOS never got the device key out of its keystore.** Follow-up to the change below, caught by dispatching the iOS release workflow as a compile check - the first time any of that Swift/ObjC had been through a compiler. Two defects, both silent: `KeystorePlugin.swift` had a `guard` body that fell through, which is not valid Swift and killed the build outright; and the two background readers pulled the key out of the Keychain with `String(data:encoding:.utf8)`, while `storeKeyBytes` writes the RAW 32 bytes (it base64-decodes its argument, which is why `getKeyBytes` re-encodes on the way out). Random key bytes are almost never valid UTF-8, so the reader returned nil, the key arrived empty and every push fell back to generic text - exactly the failure this work exists to remove. The iOS migration had the mirror error, writing the base64 text where the login path writes bytes, so a migrated install would have disagreed with a fresh one. Android was right on both counts. The contract - raw bytes at rest, base64 across the FFI - is now asserted across all five files by `pushContextFields.test.ts`, since nothing else checks it off macOS
- **The MLS device key no longer sits in cleartext application data.** `push_context.json` held `deviceKeyB64` - the single key that decrypts `mls.bin` and every local message - as plain text, because a background FCM/NSE handler runs with no user present and so cannot satisfy a biometric prompt. The requirement was real; the plaintext file was not the way to meet it, and on iOS that file is swept up by an unencrypted Finder/iTunes backup. The key now lives only in the platform keystore. Android needed no new key: the existing alias is already `setUserAuthenticationRequired(false)`, so the gap was a Context-only reader (`MlsDeviceKeyStore`) - the plugin's own accessor demands an Activity for a purely cosmetic prompt. It deliberately does **not** set `setUnlockedDeviceRequired(true)`, which would make the key unusable while the screen is locked, i.e. exactly when a push arrives. iOS did need a second keychain item (`mls_bg_key_<alias>`, `AfterFirstUnlockThisDeviceOnly`, no access control, shared with the NSE via access group `group.fr.emse.canari`) because the existing one is `.userPresence`-gated and an extension can never satisfy that. A one-shot migration promotes the key at the next app start and strips the field, including the App Group mirror; the background readers have no JSON fallback, so at most one push before that launch shows generic text

### Fixed
- **Every deep link reached the right tab and then opened nothing.** A shared community link, a tapped message or channel notification, an accepted invitation - all of them landed on the right route with "Aucun echange selectionne", or on whichever community sorted first. Routing was not the problem: the target *was* selected, and then lost. A deep link was attempted exactly once - `openNotificationTarget` reported success and the pending target was released immediately - while the conversations map it selects into is emptied and rebuilt wholesale by the IndexedDB restore and pruned on every community refetch. Any of those landing a moment later left the selection pointing at a key the map no longer had, and the selection watchdog in `useConversations` dutifully nulled it, with nothing left to retry from. Worse, the wipe cascaded: with `selectedContact` already null, the route-mode reset concluded the selection did not belong to `/communities` and cleared the community the sidebar reveals too, which is how an invite link surfaced a completely different community. The target is now held until it is actually displayed, so the landing re-asserts itself instead of losing the race, and the watchdog keeps a selection that is still arriving. Holding is only half of it - the landing also has to know when to stop, or a revoked channel would pin a selection forever: it refetches the communities once for a channel it does not know, retries when that refetch was dropped by the loader's in-flight guard (a join racing the startup load waited on a refresh nobody performed), and abandons the target when a real refresh still does not find it, or when a DM is missing from an already-restored map. `MainChatPage` no longer runs a second copy of the landing: it reads the very same global stores as `ChatBackgroundService` and only served to release the target early
- **iOS decrypted no push notification at all, and had not since v0.11.0.** `canari_push.mm` and the notification service extension both parsed `json["pin"]` out of `push_context.json` and rejected the context when that field was empty - but the v0.11.0 rename made `store_push_context` write `deviceKeyB64`, and nothing has written `pin` since. `CanariLoadPushContext` therefore returned nil on *every* call: every push served the generic fallback, and quick reply, mark-read, welcome-request and the outbox drain all aborted at "push_context absent". The value was never wrong, only the key name - the Rust FFI parameter was already `device_key_b64`. Android was unaffected. Nothing could catch this: the contract is a JSON string key on one side and a string literal on three others, so Rust, Swift, ObjC and Kotlin all compiled happily. `pushContextFields.test.ts` now cross-checks what `store_push_context` writes against what all three native readers parse, and fails on drift
- **The community settings modal was unreadable in dark mode.** Its panels, cards, member list, invite row and inputs were painted with one-way light-mode classes (`bg-white`, `bg-white/50`, `bg-amber-100 text-amber-900`, `bg-red-50 text-red-600`, a `color-mix(var(--cn-surface), white)` sidebar) while the text on top of them used `text-text-main`, which correctly flips to near-white - white text on a white card. Only the text obeyed the theme, so the modal degraded exactly where the theme changed. The whole modal now draws from the `app.css` tokens (`bg-cn-surface`, `bg-cn-bg`, `text-red-err`, `text-green-ok`, `bg-cn-yellow`/`text-cn-ink`), which flip on their own. `--color-cn-surface` and `--color-surface-elevated` are new `@theme` entries so `bg-cn-surface` exists as a utility instead of every component reaching for `bg-(--cn-surface)` or, worse, `bg-white`
- **The biometric sheets hardcoded their surfaces** (`bg-white/95 dark:bg-[#1a1f2e]/95`, where `#1a1f2e` is not even `--cn-surface`), pinning the enrolment and unlock sheets to a colour pair maintained by hand. Both now use `bg-cn-surface/95` and token borders. The `dark:` variant itself is sound on the web build - verified by computed style in both themes - so a sheet reported dark under a light theme on device points at the native runtime, not at these classes
- **"Moderer les messages" drove nothing.** The community role matrix advertised the permission per role - "pin or delete other members' messages" - but no channel message could be deleted by anyone, admin included: `MainChatPage` passed `onDelete`/`onEdit` as `undefined` for every channel, and the backend had no delete endpoint at all. Pinning, meanwhile, ignored the permission entirely and let any member pin anyone's message. `DELETE /api/channels/:channelId/messages/:messageId` now exists (author always, someone else's with `channel.moderate`), pinning someone else's message requires the same permission, and `memberCanModerateMessages` is the one check every moderation path shares, so the matrix and the enforcement cannot drift. The workspace listing gained `viewerCanModerate` so the client can offer the action without probing for a 403
- **System messages named everyone but their author "Utilisateur inconnu".** `resolveDisplayNames` gated its fetch on `getUserDisplayNameSync(id) !== id`, but that helper answers a cache miss with the localized "unknown user" label - never the id - so the guard always passed and the fetch below was unreachable. Only the current user resolved, via the separate `currentUserId()` branch. Inviting someone to a community therefore posted "X a ajouté Utilisateur inconnu au groupe" into the channel, and because system-message text is composed client-side and stored server-side, the wrong name was permanent. Resolution now goes through a new `peekUserDisplayName`, which returns `null` on a miss instead of inventing a placeholder
- **A device that rejoined a group by external commit received nothing afterwards.** `validateCommit` advanced the epoch and fanned the commit out to the existing members, but never created the joining device's `DeviceGroupMembership` row - the external commit is the one join path with no Welcome, so nothing else creates it. Recipient resolution filters on `status='active'`, so the rejoined device was invisible to routing while believing it was a member: its own messages went out, but the history bundle it solicited and every subsequent live message were fanned out to everyone except it. Observed end to end on a wiped device: the peer logged `Full history sent: 33 message(s)` three times over three minutes and the requester never saw a single frame. `validateCommit` now promotes the committing device to `active` when it has no active row

## [v0.11.2] - 2026-07-28

### Fixed
- **No MLS state could be saved through the encrypt worker since v0.11.0.** The PIN -> deviceKey rename updated the worker's reader (`payload.deviceKeyB64`) but left the sender posting `payload.pin`, so the worker sealed with `undefined` and wasm-bindgen died reading `undefined.length`. Harmless-looking on the checkpoint paths, where the rejection was only logged - fatal on the fresh-start path, which awaits the save and turned it into `can't access property "length", e is undefined` at login. The three MLS worker contracts (encrypt, catch-up decrypt, key packages) now live in one `mlsWorkerProtocol.ts` imported by both ends, so a renamed field fails `svelte-check` instead of arriving as `undefined`
- **Every install that predated v0.11.0 was locked out of its own messages.** v0.11.0 changed the at-rest envelope of the MLS snapshot (Argon2id + 16-byte salt prefix, keyed on the PIN -> ChaCha20-Poly1305, keyed on the PBKDF2 device key) without shipping a reader for the old one, and neither the MLS IndexedDB (pinned at schema version 1) nor native `mls.bin` was ever versioned - so the old blobs stayed exactly where they were. The first v0.11.x login could not decrypt them and reported "your PIN was changed on another device", offering a recovery that no PIN could satisfy. Both platforms now try the legacy envelope once with the PIN just verified, then re-seal and persist the snapshot under the device key, so local history survives the upgrade. Opening the envelope says nothing about whose state it is: a snapshot left by an interrupted fresh start names the previous device, and that verdict is re-read after the migration so it takes the fresh start it needs instead of escaping init as a raw "Credential identity mismatch"
- **Native MLS was entirely dead on mobile and desktop since v0.11.0.** `TauriMlsService` invoked `initialiser_mls_avec_clef`, `sauvegarder_mls_et_persister_avec_clef` and `generer_key_packages_et_persister_avec_clef`; the Rust commands are registered as `initialiser_mls`, `sauvegarder_mls_et_persister` and `generer_key_packages_et_persister`. Every native MLS init, state save and KeyPackage publication failed with "command not found" - which also meant no Tauri device published a KeyPackage and none appeared under "Connected devices"
- **Biometric sessions could never save their MLS state.** The at-rest key stays in the platform keystore and never reaches JS, so every save and KeyPackage batch arrived with an empty `deviceKeyB64` and was rejected by `decode_base64_to_32_bytes`. `initialiser_mls` now resolves the key once via `MlsManager::resolve_at_rest_key` and caches it in `AppState`, keeping the BiometricPrompt to one per session
- **A credential identity mismatch was reported as "your PIN was changed on another device".** The two failures are now separated by `BaseMlsService.classifyStateLoadFailure`: only a state that will not decrypt pauses for the old-PIN recovery. A mismatch decrypted fine and no PIN can repair it, so it goes straight to a fresh start instead of stranding the user in a recovery they cannot complete
- **The fresh-start branch left the old state on disk**, so the new device id and the stored blob disagreed and the next launch mismatched again - four device ids minted and deleted in eight seconds in production. The fresh state is now persisted before anything else can fail
- **`/settings` and `/login` could bounce off each other indefinitely**, roughly one round trip per second with a token refresh each. The page guard required MLS readiness while the login page only checked the OIDC session, so the two never converged; `/settings` now tests for an account session, and the login page refuses a second identical auto-redirect within 5 seconds
- **Every session minted a new device id and deleted the previous one server-side.** `WebMlsService.init` and `TauriMlsService.init` redeclared the override without the `opts` parameter, silently dropping `noFreshStart`; an undecryptable local state then took the destructive fresh-start branch instead of surfacing the recoverable "state sealed with the old key" signal. The Tauri override also never cleared `initPromise` on failure, so a retry after recovery would have replayed the cached rejection - it now inherits `BaseMlsService.init`

### Changed
- PIN policy simplified back to a single rule - at least 4 characters - applied identically on setup, change, recovery and unlock. The 4-to-8-digit creation rule added in v0.11.1 is gone, along with the numpad and input caps it implied
